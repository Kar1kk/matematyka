Solving triangles: the law of sines and the law of cosines

The law of cosines is Pythagoras with a correction $-2ab\cos\gamma$: for an acute angle the side is shorter, for an obtuse one longer than "by Pythagoras". The law of sines says that a side divided by the sine of the opposite angle is always the diameter of the circumscribed circle.

VII.3, VII.R.7, VII.R.8, VIII.2

1. Theorems

Notation: sides $a,b,c$ lie opposite the angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ (at vertices $A,B,C$), $R$ is the circumradius.

Area of a triangle on CKE sheet $$P=\frac12ab\sin\gamma$$
Proof (required on the exam)

$P=\frac12a\cdot h$, where $h$ is the altitude from $A$ to the line $BC$ and $D$ is its foot. In the right triangle $ADC$ the hypotenuse is $b$.

  • $\gamma$ acute: the angle at $C$ in triangle $ADC$ is $\gamma$, so $h=b\sin\gamma$.
  • $\gamma$ obtuse: $D$ lies on the extension of $BC$ beyond $C$, the angle at $C$ is $180^\circ-\gamma$, so $h=b\sin(180^\circ-\gamma)=b\sin\gamma$.
  • $\gamma=90^\circ$: $h=b=b\sin90^\circ$.

Always $h=b\sin\gamma$, so $P=\frac12ab\sin\gamma$. $\blacksquare$

Law of cosines on CKE sheet $$c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma$$
Proof (required on the exam)

Place the triangle in a coordinate system: $C=(0,0)$, $B=(a,0)$. Point $A$ lies on the ray of angle $\gamma$ at distance $b$ from $C$, so by the definition of trigonometric functions (valid for every $\gamma\in(0^\circ,180^\circ)$): $A=(b\cos\gamma,\,b\sin\gamma)$. Then

$$c^2=|AB|^2=(b\cos\gamma-a)^2+b^2\sin^2\gamma$$ $$=b^2(\cos^2\gamma+\sin^2\gamma)-2ab\cos\gamma+a^2$$ $$=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma\quad\blacksquare$$
Converse of the Pythagorean theorem If $c^2=a^2+b^2$, then $\gamma=90^\circ$. More generally: $$c^2<a^2+b^2\iff\gamma\text{ acute}$$ $$c^2>a^2+b^2\iff\gamma\text{ obtuse}$$
Proof (required on the exam)

From the law of cosines $a^2+b^2-c^2=2ab\cos\gamma$, and $2ab>0$. So the sign of $a^2+b^2-c^2$ is the sign of $\cos\gamma$. When $c^2=a^2+b^2$, $\cos\gamma=0$, and the only such angle in $(0^\circ,180^\circ)$ is $90^\circ$. Similarly $\cos\gamma>0\iff\gamma$ acute, $\cos\gamma<0\iff\gamma$ obtuse. $\blacksquare$

The largest angle lies opposite the longest side (VIII.2), so the type of the triangle is decided by comparing the square of the longest side with the sum of the squares of the other two.

Law of sines on CKE sheet $$\frac{a}{\sin\alpha}=\frac{b}{\sin\beta}=\frac{c}{\sin\gamma}=2R$$
Proof (required on the exam)

It suffices to show $a=2R\sin\alpha$ (the same for $b$, $c$). Let $O$ be the center of the circumscribed circle and $BD$ its diameter, $|BD|=2R$. Angle $BCD$ is inscribed and subtends a diameter, so $\angle BCD=90^\circ$ and $a=|BC|=2R\sin\angle BDC$.

  • $\alpha$ acute: $A$ and $D$ lie on the same side of $BC$, the inscribed angles $BAC$ and $BDC$ subtend the same arc: $\angle BDC=\alpha$, so $a=2R\sin\alpha$.
  • $\alpha$ obtuse: $A$ and $D$ lie on opposite sides of $BC$, the quadrilateral $ABDC$ is inscribed in a circle: $\angle BDC=180^\circ-\alpha$, so $a=2R\sin(180^\circ-\alpha)=2R\sin\alpha$.
  • $\alpha=90^\circ$: $BC$ is a diameter, $a=2R=2R\sin90^\circ$. $\blacksquare$
A consequence of both formulas: $\sin\gamma=\frac{c}{2R}$, so $P=\frac{abc}{4R}$ on CKE sheet.

4. Step-by-step example

In a triangle $a=5$, $b=8$, $\gamma=60^\circ$. Find $c$, the area and the circumradius.

  1. $c^2=25+64-2\cdot5\cdot8\cdot\frac12=49$, so $c=7$.
  2. $P=\frac12\cdot5\cdot8\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2=10\sqrt3$.
  3. $2R=\frac{c}{\sin\gamma}=\frac{7}{\sqrt3/2}=\frac{14}{\sqrt3}$, so $R=\frac{7\sqrt3}{3}$.

$c=7,\ P=10\sqrt3,\ R=\frac{7\sqrt3}{3}$

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    Decide whether the triangle with sides a) $4,5,6$, b) $4,5,7$ is acute, right or obtuse.
    Solution
    Compare the square of the longest side with the sum of the squares of the other two: $16+25=41$. a) $36<41$; b) $49>41$. a) acute, b) obtuse
  2. easy
    Find the area of the triangle in which $a=6$, $b=10$, $\gamma=150^\circ$.
    Solution
    $P=\frac12\cdot6\cdot10\cdot\sin150^\circ=30\cdot\frac12$. $P=15$
  3. medium
    The sides of a triangle have lengths $7$, $8$, $13$. Find the measure of the largest angle.
    Solution
    The largest angle lies opposite the side $13$: $\cos\gamma=\frac{49+64-169}{2\cdot7\cdot8}=\frac{-56}{112}=-\frac12$. $\gamma=120^\circ$
  4. medium
    In a triangle $a=6$, $\alpha=30^\circ$, $\beta=45^\circ$. Find $b$ and the circumradius.
    Solution
    $2R=\frac{6}{\sin30^\circ}=12$, $b=2R\sin45^\circ=12\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}2$. $b=6\sqrt2$, $R=6$
  5. exam-level
    In triangle $ABC$: $|BC|=2$, $|AC|=2\sqrt3$, $\angle BAC=30^\circ$. Find the remaining angles (consider all possibilities).
    Solution
    Law of sines: $\frac{2}{\sin30^\circ}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{\sin\beta}$, so $\sin\beta=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\beta=60^\circ$ or $\beta=120^\circ$. Both are possible, since $30^\circ+120^\circ<180^\circ$ (here $a<b$, so $\alpha<\beta$ – no contradiction). $\beta=60^\circ,\ \gamma=90^\circ$ or $\beta=120^\circ,\ \gamma=30^\circ$
  6. exam-level
    A parallelogram has sides $4$ and $6$ and an acute angle of $60^\circ$. Find the lengths of its diagonals and its area.
    Solution
    The shorter diagonal lies opposite the $60^\circ$ angle: $d_1^2=16+36-2\cdot4\cdot6\cdot\frac12=28$. The longer one opposite $120^\circ$: $d_2^2=16+36+24=76$. Area $=4\cdot6\cdot\sin60^\circ$. $d_1=2\sqrt7$, $d_2=2\sqrt{19}$, $P=12\sqrt3$

6. On the exam

  • An angle found from a sine has two values ($\beta$ and $180^\circ-\beta$). Check both: angle sum $<180^\circ$ and the larger angle opposite the longer side. An angle found from a cosine is unique.
  • When identifying the type of a triangle, compare with the longest side – one check is enough.
  • In the law of cosines the angle must lie between the sides $a$ and $b$, with $c$ opposite it. For an obtuse angle $-2ab\cos\gamma$ is positive – watch the sign.
  • Proofs of the law of sines, the law of cosines and the area formula are required – the exam may contain a "prove that" task.
