Logarithms (with change of base)

$\log_a b$ answers the question: how many times must you multiply $a$ by itself to get $b$? $\log_2 8=3$, because $2\cdot2\cdot2=8$. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition — hence all the formulas.

I.1, I.9, I.R (change of base) · proof: properties of logarithms

1. Definition

Logarithm on CKE sheet
$$\log_a b=c\iff a^c=b$$ $$a^{\log_a b}=b$$
conditions
$a>0$, $a\ne1$, $b>0$
$\lg b$
$=\log b=\log_{10}b$ on CKE sheet
always
$\log_a1=0$, $\log_a a=1$, $\log_a a^c=c$
Where do the conditions come from? $a=1$: $1^c=1$ for every $c$, so “how many times” makes no sense. $a<0$: $(-2)^{1/2}$ does not exist. $b\le0$: $a^c>0$ always, so you cannot get $b\le0$.

2. Interactive: a multiplication counter

The vertical axis is logarithmic: each multiplication by $a$ is a step of the same height. The number of steps up to the level $b$ is $\log_a b$; the last, green step may be fractional. Choose $a<1$ or $b<1$: the steps go left or down.

3. Formulas and proof

Operations on CKE sheet
$$\log_a(xy)=\log_a x+\log_a y$$ $$\log_a\frac xy=\log_a x-\log_a y$$ $$\log_a x^r=r\log_a x$$
assumptions
$a>0$, $a\ne1$, $x,y>0$, $r\in\R$
Theorem The three formulas above are true.
Proof (required on the exam)

Let $u=\log_a x$ and $v=\log_a y$. By definition $a^u=x$, $a^v=y$. We use the laws of powers:

$$xy=a^u a^v=a^{u+v}$$ $$\frac xy=\frac{a^u}{a^v}=a^{u-v}$$ $$x^r=(a^u)^r=a^{ru}$$

By the definition of a logarithm (the exponent to which we raise $a$): $\log_a(xy)=u+v$, $\log_a\frac xy=u-v$, $\log_a x^r=ru$. These are exactly the three formulas. $\blacksquare$

Change of base, extended level on CKE sheet
$$\log_b c=\frac{\log_a c}{\log_a b},\qquad \log_a b=\frac{1}{\log_b a}$$
assumptions

4. Interactive: a logarithm turns multiplication into addition

On a logarithmic axis the number $x$ lies at distance $\lg x$ from one. Drag $x$ and $y$: the segment $\lg y$ attached at the end of $\lg x$ lands exactly on $xy$. This is how the slide rule worked. For $y<1$ the segment goes left (division).

5. Computing and comparing

  • Common base: $\log_{\sqrt2}8=c\iff (2^{1/2})^c=2^3\iff c=6$; $\;\log_{1/3}9=-2$.
  • Estimating with integers: $4<5<8$, so $2<\log_2 5<3$; $\;3<8<9$, so $1<\log_3 8<2$. Hence $\log_2 5>\log_3 8$.
  • The same base: for $a>1$ the larger number has the larger logarithm, for $0<a<1$ the other way round: $\log_{0.5}3>\log_{0.5}5$.

6. Step-by-step example

Compute $\log_3 18-\log_3 2+\log_2\sqrt[3]{4}$.

  1. Difference of logarithms: $\log_3\frac{18}{2}=\log_3 9=2$.
  2. $\sqrt[3]4=2^{2/3}$, so $\log_2 2^{2/3}=\frac23$.

$2+\frac23=\frac83$

(R) Compute $\log_4 27\cdot\log_9 2$.

  1. $\log_4 27=\log_{2^2}3^3=\frac32\log_2 3$, $\;\log_9 2=\frac12\log_3 2$.
  2. $\log_2 3\cdot\log_3 2=1$ (change of base).

$\frac32\cdot\frac12=\frac34$

7. Tasks

  1. easy
    Compute $\log_2\frac18+\log_5\sqrt5+\log1000$.
    Solution
    $-3+\frac12+3=$ $\frac12$
  2. easy
    For which $x$ does $\log_{x-1}(5-x)$ exist?
    Solution
    $x-1>0$, $x-1\ne1$, $5-x>0$, i.e. $x>1$, $x\ne2$, $x<5$. $x\in(1,2)\cup(2,5)$
  3. medium
    Compute: a) $\log_6 9+2\log_6 2$, b) $9^{\log_3 5}$.
    Solution
    a) $\log_6 9+\log_6 4=\log_6 36=$ $2$. b) $9^{\log_3 5}=(3^{\log_3 5})^2=5^2=$ $25$.
  4. medium
    Which number is greater: $\log_3 10$ or $\log_4 15$?
    Solution
    $10>9=3^2$, so $\log_3 10>2$; $15<16=4^2$, so $\log_4 15<2$. $\log_3 10>\log_4 15$
  5. exam-level
    (R) It is known that $\log_2 3=a$. Express $\log_{12}18$ in terms of $a$.
    Solution
    $\log_{12}18=\frac{\log_2 18}{\log_2 12}=\frac{\log_2(2\cdot3^2)}{\log_2(2^2\cdot3)}=$ $\frac{1+2a}{2+a}$
  6. exam-level
    (R) Show that $\log_2 3\cdot\log_3 4\cdot\log_4 5\cdot\ldots\cdot\log_{31}32=5$.
    Solution
    Change to base $2$: $\log_k(k+1)=\frac{\log_2(k+1)}{\log_2 k}$. The product cancels “telescopically”: $$\frac{\log_2 3}{\log_2 2}\cdot\frac{\log_2 4}{\log_2 3}\cdots\frac{\log_2 32}{\log_2 31}$$ What remains is $\frac{\log_2 32}{\log_2 2}=$ $5$, which completes the proof.

8. On the exam

  • Domain first: the base $>0$ and $\ne1$, the argument $>0$. Missing assumptions = a lost point.
  • There is no formula for $\log_a(x+y)$. $\;\log_a(x+y)\ne\log_a x+\log_a y$.
  • Trap: $\log_a x^2=2\log_a|x|$, not $2\log_a x$, when $x$ may be negative.
  • Comparing: estimate between consecutive integers; for a base in $(0,1)$ reverse the inequality.
  • The formulas for logarithms and change of base are on the formula sheet, but you must be able to reproduce the proof of the formula for the logarithm of a product.
