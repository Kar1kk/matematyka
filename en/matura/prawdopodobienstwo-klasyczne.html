Classical probability

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability is plain counting: how many outcomes favour the event, divided by how many there are in total. The whole difficulty lies in counting correctly, that is, in combinatorics.

1. The classical model

Definition $\Omega$ is a finite set of elementary events, each of which is equally likely. For an event $A\subset\Omega$: $$P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}$$
Properties on CKE sheet
$$0\le P(A)\le 1,\qquad P(A')=1-P(A)$$ $$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$
$A'$
the complementary event: $A$ did not occur. Worth using when the statement says “at least one”
$A\cap B$
the intersection, counted twice in $P(A)+P(B)$, so you subtract it once

2. Counting: rules and formulas

Multiplication rule: a procedure made of stages with $k_1, k_2,\ldots$ possibilities has $k_1\cdot k_2\cdot\ldots$ outcomes. Addition rule: you add disjoint cases.

Permutations (arranging all $n$ in order): $n!$. All these formulas are on the formula sheet.

Permutations with repetition (e.g. anagrams of a word with repeated letters): if among $n$ elements there are $k_1$ identical ones of one kind, $k_2$ of another, etc., then the number of arrangements is $\frac{n!}{k_1!\,k_2!\cdots}$. E.g. the word MAMA ($n=4$, two M's, two A's): $\frac{4!}{2!\,2!}=6$.

Binomial coefficient
$$\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$$ $$\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k},\qquad \binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$
Where does $\binom nk$ come from? (arrangements divided by $k!$)
The number of sequences of $k$ distinct elements out of $n$ is $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$. Each $k$-element set gives $k!$ such sequences (its permutations). So the number of sets is $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!\,k!}$.
Drawing “all at once” can be counted with combinations, or as drawing “one after another” with arrangements. The result is the same, as long as you count $\Omega$ and $A$ in the same way.

5. Step-by-step example

An urn contains $5$ white balls and $3$ black ones. We draw $2$ balls at once. Find the probability that both are of the same colour.

  1. $\Omega$: two-element subsets of the $8$ balls, $|\Omega|=\binom82=28$.
  2. $A$: two white or two black (disjoint, so we add): $|A|=\binom52+\binom32=10+3=13$.
  3. $P(A)=\frac{13}{28}$ (an irreducible fraction).

$P(A)=\frac{13}{28}$

6. Tasks

  1. easy
    A die is rolled twice. Find the probability that the sum of the pips equals $8$.
    Solution
    $|\Omega|=36$, $A=\{(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)\}$. $P(A)=\frac{5}{36}$
  2. easy
    One number is drawn from the set $\{1,2,\ldots,30\}$. Find the probability that it is divisible by $3$ or by $5$.
    Solution
    Divisible by $3$: $10$, by $5$: $6$, by $15$ (both at once): $2$. $|A\cup B|=10+6-2=14$. $P=\frac{14}{30}=\frac{7}{15}$
  3. medium
    A four-digit number is drawn. Find the probability that all its digits are different.
    Solution
    $|\Omega|=9000$. First digit: $9$ possibilities (not $0$), second: $9$ (now including $0$, excluding the first), third: $8$, fourth: $7$. $|A|=9\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7=4536$. $P=\frac{4536}{9000}=\frac{63}{125}$
  4. medium
    Ten people, including Anna and Bob, line up in a queue at random. Find the probability that Anna and Bob stand next to each other.
    Solution
    $|\Omega|=10!$. Glue Anna and Bob into one “block”: you arrange $9$ objects in $9!$ ways, and the block in $2$ ways (AB or BA). $|A|=2\cdot9!$. $P=\frac{2\cdot9!}{10!}=\frac{1}{5}$
  5. exam-level
    A box contains $6$ white balls and $4$ black ones. We draw $3$ balls at once. Find the probability that at least one of them is black.
    Solution
    “At least one” → the complementary event $A'$: all white. $|\Omega|=\binom{10}3=120$, $|A'|=\binom63=20$. $P(A)=1-\frac{20}{120}$. $P(A)=\frac{5}{6}$
  6. exam-level
    The number $n$ is even. Two numbers are drawn at once from the set $\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$. The probability that both are even equals $\frac{7}{30}$. Find $n$.
    Solution
    There are $\frac n2$ even numbers. $P=\frac{\binom{n/2}{2}}{\binom n2}=\frac{\frac n2\left(\frac n2-1\right)}{n(n-1)}=\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}$. The equation $\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}=\frac{7}{30}$ gives $30n-60=28n-28$, $n=16$. Check: $\frac{\binom82}{\binom{16}2}=\frac{28}{120}=\frac{7}{30}$. $n=16$

7. On the exam

  • Always describe $\Omega$ and give $|\Omega|$ (e.g. “$\Omega$: two-element sequences from $\{1,\ldots,6\}$, $|\Omega|=36$”). Without it, it is safer not to count on full marks.
  • Give the result as an irreducible fraction (or an exact decimal). An approximation without being asked for it is an error.
  • “At least one”, “at most $n-1$” → compute $P(A')$.
  • A consistent model: do not count $\Omega$ with combinations and $A$ with arrangements. With dice, the outcomes $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are two different elementary events.
  • Four-digit numbers: the first digit $\neq0$. For digits like “even and without repetition” split into cases (last digit $0$ or not).
