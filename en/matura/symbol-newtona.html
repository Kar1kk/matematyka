Binomial coefficients in counting problems

$\binom nk$ is the number of ways to choose $k$ elements from $n$. You can prove almost every property of it without calculations: count the same thing in two ways.

XI.R.2, II.R.4, II.R.5

1. Definition and properties

Binomial coefficient on CKE sheet
$$\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$$ $$\binom nk=\frac{n(n-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(n-k+1)}{k!}$$
$0\le k\le n$
integers. The second formula on CKE sheet is handy for computing: $k$ factors in the numerator and in the denominator
property on CKE sheetwhy, in plain words
$\binom n0=\binom nn=1$empty set / whole set: one way
$\binom n1=\binom n{n-1}=n$choosing one = choosing one to leave out
$\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}$choosing $k$ to take = choosing $n-k$ to leave behind
$\binom nk+\binom n{k+1}=\binom{n+1}{k+1}$Pascal's triangle (proof below)

Also useful not on CKE sheet: $\binom n0+\binom n1+\ldots+\binom nn=2^n$, because it is the number of all subsets (task 6).

2. Pascal's triangle

Theorem If $0<k<n$, then $$\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}$$ This is the same equality as in the tables, only with $n-1$ in place of $n$ and $k-1$ in place of $k$.
Combinatorial proof (required on the exam)

Take a set of $n$ people and single out one person, call them $X$. Let us count the $k$-person subsets in two ways.

  • There are $\binom nk$ of them in total (definition).
  • Split them into two disjoint groups:
    those containing $X$: the other $k-1$ people are chosen from $n-1$, giving $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ ways;
    those not containing $X$: all $k$ people are chosen from $n-1$, giving $\binom{n-1}{k}$ ways.

Every subset is in exactly one group, so by the addition rule $\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}$. The assumption $0<k<n$ guarantees that both symbols on the right make sense ($0\le k-1$ and $k\le n-1$). $\blacksquare$

This is why in Pascal's triangle every number is the sum of the two numbers above it, and the edges are all ones. Row $n$ is $\binom n0,\binom n1,\ldots,\binom nn$.

Click a number in the triangle (or use the sliders). Red = $\binom nk$, blue = the two numbers above it. The ▶ button builds the triangle row by row.

3. The binomial theorem

Binomial theorem on CKE sheet For $n\ge 1$ and any $a,b\in\R$: $$(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k$$ that is, $\binom n0a^n+\binom n1a^{n-1}b+\ldots+\binom nn b^n$. Substituting $-b$ for $b$ gives the formula for $(a-b)^n$ on CKE sheet: alternating signs, $(-1)^k\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k$.
Combinatorial proof (required on the exam)

$(a+b)^n=\underbrace{(a+b)(a+b)\cdots(a+b)}_{n\text{ brackets}}$. When expanding, from each bracket you take $a$ or $b$ and multiply the chosen letters. Each of the $2^n$ such choices gives one term.

The term $a^{n-k}b^k$ arises exactly when $b$ was taken from $k$ brackets and $a$ from the remaining $n-k$. Such a choice is determined by the set of $k$ brackets from which you take $b$, and there are $\binom nk$ such sets. After collecting like terms, the coefficient of $a^{n-k}b^k$ is therefore $\binom nk$. $\blacksquare$

(You can also use induction: $(a+b)^{n+1}=(a+b)^n(a+b)$, and the coefficients add up as in Pascal's triangle.)

Set $n$ and $k$, then click ▶. The animation goes through all choices of $k$ brackets from which you take $b$ (red). Each choice gives one $a^{n-k}b^k$, so the bar grows up to $\binom nk$. The bars are row $n$ of Pascal's triangle.

4. Formulas for $a^n\pm b^n$ and divisibility

Theorem For $n\ge 1$ on CKE sheet: $$\begin{aligned}a^n-b^n=(a-b)(&a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b\\&+\ldots+b^{n-1})\end{aligned}$$ For odd $n$ (not in the tables, except $n=3$): $$\begin{aligned}a^n+b^n=(a+b)(&a^{n-1}-a^{n-2}b\\&+\ldots+b^{n-1})\end{aligned}$$
Proof (required on the exam)

Multiply out the right-hand side of the first formula. $a$ times the bracket gives $a^n+a^{n-1}b+\ldots+ab^{n-1}$, and $-b$ times the bracket gives $-a^{n-1}b-\ldots-ab^{n-1}-b^n$. All the middle terms cancel (a “telescoping” sum), leaving $a^n-b^n$.

Second formula: substitute $-b$ for $b$. For odd $n$ we have $(-b)^n=-b^n$, so the left-hand side is $a^n+b^n$, and in the bracket the signs start to alternate. $\blacksquare$

Corollary For integers $a\ne b$ and $n\ge 1$: $\;(a-b)\mid(a^n-b^n)$.
Proof (required on the exam)

Method 1. From the first formula $a^n-b^n=(a-b)\cdot m$, where $m=a^{n-1}+\ldots+b^{n-1}$ is an integer (a sum of products of integers).

Method 2 (from the binomial theorem). Let $d=a-b$. Then $a^n=(b+d)^n=b^n+\binom n1b^{n-1}d+\ldots+d^n$, so

$$a^n-b^n=d\cdot\Big(\tbinom n1 b^{n-1}+\ldots+d^{n-1}\Big)$$

and the bracket is an integer. $\blacksquare$

Examples: $5\mid 7^n-2^n$; $\;3\mid 2^{100}-1$, because $2^{100}-1=4^{50}-1^{50}$ and $4-1=3$. From the second formula: for odd $n$ we have $(a+b)\mid(a^n+b^n)$, e.g. $9\mid 4^{7}+5^{7}$.

5. Step-by-step example

Expand $(x-2)^4$.

  1. Row $4$ of Pascal's triangle: $1,\,4,\,6,\,4,\,1$.
  2. $a=x$, $b=-2$: the powers of $b$ are $1,\,-2,\,4,\,-8,\,16$.
  3. Multiply in pairs: $1\cdot x^4$, $4\cdot(-2)x^3$, $6\cdot 4x^2$, $4\cdot(-8)x$, $1\cdot 16$.

$x^4-8x^3+24x^2-32x+16$

How many shortest paths along grid lines are there from $(0,0)$ to $(5,3)$ (steps: right or up)?

  1. Every path has $8$ steps: $5$ to the right and $3$ up.
  2. A path is determined by the set of numbers of the “up” steps: $\binom83=\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6}{6}$.

$56$ paths

This is Pascal's triangle again: you reach each point from the left or from below, so the numbers of paths add up.

6. Tasks

  1. easy
    Calculate $\binom{10}{3}+\binom{10}{4}$ and compare it with $\binom{11}{4}$.
    Solution
    $\binom{10}3=\frac{10\cdot9\cdot8}{6}=120$, $\binom{10}4=\binom{10}{3}\cdot\frac74=210$. The sum is $330$, and by Pascal's property it equals $\binom{11}{4}=\frac{11\cdot10\cdot9\cdot8}{24}=330$. $330$, equal
  2. easy
    Find the coefficient of $x^3$ in the expansion of $(2x-1)^5$.
    Solution
    The term with $x^3$: $\binom53(2x)^3(-1)^2=10\cdot 8x^3$. $80$
  3. medium
    A convex polygon has $35$ diagonals. How many sides does it have?
    Solution
    The number of segments joining $n$ vertices is $\binom n2$, of which $n$ are sides: $\binom n2-n=\frac{n(n-3)}{2}=35$. Hence $n^2-3n-70=0$, $n=10$ (you reject the other root $-7$). $10$ sides
  4. medium
    Find the term independent of $x$ in the expansion of $\left(x+\frac2x\right)^6$, $x\ne 0$.
    Solution
    General term: $\binom6k x^{6-k}\left(\frac2x\right)^k=\binom6k2^k x^{6-2k}$. Independent of $x$: $6-2k=0$, $k=3$. $\binom63\cdot 8=20\cdot8$. $160$
  5. exam-level
    Prove that the number $11^{10}-1$ is divisible by $100$.
    Solution
    $11^{10}=(1+10)^{10}=1+\binom{10}{1}\cdot10+\binom{10}{2}\cdot10^2+\ldots+10^{10}$. Hence $11^{10}-1=100+\binom{10}{2}10^2+\ldots+10^{10}$: the first term is $10\cdot10=100$, and each following one contains $10^k$ with $k\ge2$. Every term is divisible by $100$, so the whole sum is too.
  6. exam-level
    Prove that $\binom n0+\binom n1+\ldots+\binom nn=2^n$ for $n\ge1$. Give two justifications.
    Solution
    Combinatorially: the left-hand side is the number of subsets of an $n$-element set, grouped by the number of elements. By the multiplication rule there are $2^n$ subsets (each element either belongs or does not). From the binomial theorem: substitute $a=b=1$: $2^n=(1+1)^n=\sum_k\binom nk$. The claim holds.

7. On the exam

  • Combinatorial proofs: name the set you are counting and say into which disjoint parts you split it. Without this the proof is not complete.
  • General term $\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k$: write it out explicitly, then compare exponents. Watch the sign when $b$ is negative.
  • The expansion of $(a+b)^n$ has $n+1$ terms, not $n$.
  • Divisibility such as $11^{10}-1$, $7^n-2^n$: expand the binomial or use $(a-b)\mid(a^n-b^n)$ and cite it directly.
  • Equations with $\binom n2$, $\binom n3$: turn them into a polynomial and reject solutions that are not natural numbers $\ge k$.
