Geometric series

Infinitely many terms can give a finite sum: $\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\ldots$ fills a square of area $1$. This works exactly when $|q|<1$, that is, when the successive pieces shrink exponentially.

VI.R.2

1. Definition and formula

A geometric series is an “infinite sum” $a_1+a_1q+a_1q^2+\ldots$ Its sum is defined as the limit of the partial sums $S=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}S_n$, where $S_n=a_1+\ldots+a_1q^{n-1}$. If this limit exists (and is a number), the series is convergent.
Sum of a geometric series
$$S=\frac{a_1}{1-q},\qquad |q|<1$$
$|q|<1$
the series converges (for $a_1\neq0$ this is a necessary and sufficient condition)
$|q|\ge1$
the series diverges (when $a_1\neq0$): the terms do not shrink to $0$, so the partial sums cannot settle down
$a_1=0$
trivially $S=0$ for every $q$
Theorem If $|q|<1$, then the series $a_1+a_1q+a_1q^2+\ldots$ converges and $S=\frac{a_1}{1-q}$.
Proof

From the formula for the sum of a geometric sequence ($q\neq1$):

$$S_n=a_1\cdot\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}$$

For $|q|<1$ we have $q^n\to0$, so by the limit theorems:

$$\lim S_n=a_1\cdot\frac{1-0}{1-q}=\frac{a_1}{1-q}$$

For $q=1$: $S_n=na_1\to\pm\infty$. For $q=-1$: $S_n$ alternates between $a_1$ and $0$. For $|q|>1$: $|q^n|\to\infty$, so $S_n$ has no finite limit.

The formula $\frac{a_1}{1-q}$ “works” computationally even for $q=2$: $\frac{1}{1-2}=-1$, yet $1+2+4+\ldots$ is certainly not $-1$. That is why you should always check $|q|<1$ first.

2. Halves fill the square

The square has area $1$. We take a half, then half of what is left, and so on. Click ▶: an ever smaller “tail” $\frac{1}{2^n}$ remains, so the sum tends to $1$. The bar underneath shows the same thing on the segment $[0,1]$.

3. Partial sums $S_n$

The points are $(n, S_n)$, the green line is $S=\frac{a_1}{1-q}$. For $0<q<1$ the sums approach $S$ from one side, for $-1<q<0$ they jump alternately above and below $S$. Move $q$ outside $(-1,1)$: there is no limit.

4. Worked example

Convert $0.1(6)$ into a common fraction.

  1. $0.1666\ldots=\frac{1}{10}+\left(\frac{6}{100}+\frac{6}{1000}+\ldots\right)$.
  2. The bracket is a geometric series: $a_1=\frac{6}{100}$, $q=\frac1{10}$, $|q|<1$.
  3. $S=\frac{6/100}{1-1/10}=\frac{6}{90}=\frac1{15}$, so $\frac1{10}+\frac1{15}=\frac{5}{30}$.

$0.1(6)=\frac16$

Solve the equation $1+x+x^2+x^3+\ldots=\frac52-x$.

  1. The left side is a geometric series: $a_1=1$, $q=x$. It has a sum only for $|x|<1$ — this is the domain of the equation.
  2. $\frac{1}{1-x}=\frac52-x$, that is $2=(1-x)(5-2x)$, $2x^2-7x+3=0$, $x\in\{\frac12,3\}$.
  3. We reject $x=3\notin(-1,1)$. Check $x=\frac12$: $L=2$, $R=2$.

$x=\frac12$

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    Find the sum of the series $8-4+2-1+\ldots$
    Solution
    $a_1=8$, $q=-\frac12$, $|q|<1$. $S=\frac{8}{1+\frac12}=\frac{16}{3}$. $S=\frac{16}{3}$
  2. easy
    Convert $0.(27)$ into a common fraction.
    Solution
    $0.(27)=\frac{27}{100}+\frac{27}{100^2}+\ldots$, $a_1=\frac{27}{100}$, $q=\frac1{100}$. $S=\frac{27/100}{99/100}=\frac{27}{99}$. $\frac{3}{11}$
  3. medium
    For which $x$ does the series $1+(2x-1)+(2x-1)^2+\ldots$ converge? Find its sum.
    Solution
    $q=2x-1$, condition $|2x-1|<1 \iff 0<x<1$. The sum is $\frac{1}{1-(2x-1)}=\frac{1}{2-2x}$. $x\in(0,1)$, $S=\frac{1}{2-2x}$
  4. medium
    A convergent geometric series has sum $9$, and the series formed from the squares of its terms has sum $27$. Find $a_1$ and $q$.
    Solution
    The squares form a series with ratio $q^2$: $\frac{a_1}{1-q}=9$, $\frac{a_1^2}{1-q^2}=27$. Dividing the second by the first: $\frac{a_1}{1+q}=3$. Hence $9(1-q)=3(1+q)$, $q=\frac12$ ($|q|<1$ ✓), $a_1=\frac92$. $a_1=\frac92$, $q=\frac12$
  5. exam-level
    Into a square of side $1$ we inscribe a square with vertices at the midpoints of its sides, into that one another in the same way, and so on. Find the sum of the perimeters of all the squares.
    Solution
    The side of the next square is half the diagonal of the previous one: $a\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$. The perimeters: $4,\ 4\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}2,\ \ldots$, $q=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}<1$. $S=\frac{4}{1-\frac{\sqrt2}2}=\frac{8}{2-\sqrt2}=\frac{8(2+\sqrt2)}{2}$. $8+4\sqrt2$
  6. exam-level
    Solve the equation $x+\frac{x}{x+1}+\frac{x}{(x+1)^2}+\ldots=6$.
    Solution
    $a_1=x$, $q=\frac{1}{x+1}$ (of course $x\neq-1$). Convergence: $\left|\frac1{x+1}\right|<1 \iff |x+1|>1 \iff x\in(-\infty,-2)\cup(0,\infty)$. The sum: $\frac{x}{1-\frac1{x+1}}=\frac{x(x+1)}{x}=x+1$ (for $x\neq0$). The equation $x+1=6$ gives $x=5$, and $5\in(0,\infty)$ ✓. $x=5$

6. On the exam

  • Always write the condition $|q|<1$ and check every solution against it. Skipping this step is a typical lost point.
  • $q$ with a parameter: the domain of the equation is the solution of the inequality $|q|<1$ (plus conditions such as denominator $\neq0$).
  • A repeating decimal: the repeating block is $a_1$, and $q=10^{-k}$ ($k$ = length of the block). Add the part before the repeating block separately.
  • Figures going on forever (squares, triangles, circles): determine the similarity scale $k$; lengths form a series with $q=k$, and areas with $q=k^2$.
  • $0.(9)=\frac{0.9}{1-0.1}=1$ — it is the same number, not “almost $1$”.
