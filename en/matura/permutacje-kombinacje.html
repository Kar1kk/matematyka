Permutations, variations, combinations

These are not new rules, just ready-made results of the multiplication rule for common situations. The only decision: does the order matter (a sequence) and may elements repeat.

XI.R.1 (and XI.1, XI.2)

1. How to tell which formula to use

  1. Does swapping two chosen elements give a different outcome? Yes: order matters (a sequence: a code, a podium, roles on a board). No: only the set matters (a delegation, cards in a hand, drawing “at once”).
  2. Can an element repeat? Yes: the digits of a code, drawing with replacement. No: people, drawing without replacement.
  3. Do you take all the elements? Yes: that is a permutation (an arrangement in order).

2. Formulas

Permutations of $n$ elements on CKE sheet
$$P_n=n!=1\cdot 2\cdot\ldots\cdot n$$
$n!$
$n$ options for the 1st place, then $n-1$ for the 2nd… Convention: $0!=1$
Variations without repetition on CKE sheet
$$V_n^k=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$ $$=n(n-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(n-k+1)$$
$V_n^k$
sequences of $k$ distinct elements from $n$: $k$ factors, counting down from $n$
Variations with repetition on CKE sheet
$$\overline{V}{}_n^k=n^k$$
$n^k$
sequences of length $k$, with any of the $n$ elements in each place
Combinations on CKE sheet
$$C_n^k=\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$$
$\binom nk$
the number of $k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set
Why combinations are variations divided by $k!$

Every $k$-element set can be arranged into a sequence in $k!$ ways, and every sequence of distinct elements comes from exactly one set. So there are $k!$ times more sequences than sets:

$$V_n^k=\binom nk\cdot k!$$

This is exactly what you see in the animation below: a column = one set, the rows in the column = its $k!$ arrangements.

Permutations with repetition not on CKE sheet
$$\frac{n!}{k_1!\,k_2!\cdot\ldots\cdot k_s!}$$
$k_i$
how many times the $i$-th kind repeats, $k_1+\ldots+k_s=n$ (anagrams, sequences made from given digits)
Where this formula comes from (no memorizing needed)

Number the identical letters (e.g. $A_1, A_2$); then there are $n!$ arrangements in all. Each “real” word arises from $k_1!\cdot k_2!\cdots$ such arrangements (you permute the numbered copies among themselves). You divide. Alternatively: choose the places for the letters of the first kind, $\binom{n}{k_1}$, then for the second kind, $\binom{n-k_1}{k_2}$, and so on.

3. Interactive: sequences versus sets

You choose $k$ balls from $n$ colored ones. Click ▶: first all the sequences (variations) appear in alphabetical order, but each lands in the column of its set. Then one representative stays in each column: there are $k!$ times fewer sets.

4. Worked example

A group has $10$ people, including Amy. In how many ways can we choose: a) a president, a vice-president and a secretary; b) a delegation of three; c) a delegation of three that includes Amy?

  1. a) The roles are different, so swapping people changes the outcome: order matters, no repetition. $10\cdot 9\cdot 8=720$.
  2. b) A delegation has no roles: only the set matters. $\binom{10}{3}=\frac{720}{3!}=120$.
  3. c) Amy is certain; from the other $9$ people you pick $2$: $\binom 92=36$.

a) $720$, b) $120$, c) $36$

How many different “words” (anagrams) can be formed from all the letters of the (Polish) word MATEMATYKA, “mathematics”?

  1. $10$ letters: M twice, A three times, T twice, and E, Y, K once each.
  2. $\frac{10!}{2!\cdot 3!\cdot 2!}=\frac{3628800}{24}$.

$151\,200$

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    In how many ways can $6$ people sit on $6$ chairs in a row? And in how many ways if Olivia and Peter want to sit next to each other?
    Solution
    All: $6!=720$. Glue Olivia and Peter into one “block”: you arrange $5$ objects in $5!$ ways, and the block has $2$ orders. $720$ and $2\cdot 120=240$
  2. easy
    How many different strings of letters can be formed from all the letters of the word KAJAK?
    Solution
    $5$ letters: K twice, A twice, J once. $\frac{5!}{2!\,2!}=\frac{120}{4}$. $30$
  3. medium
    From a deck of $52$ cards we draw $5$ at once. How many outcomes contain exactly $2$ aces?
    Solution
    Aces: $\binom42=6$, the other $3$ cards from the $48$ non-aces: $\binom{48}{3}=17296$. Multiplication rule. $6\cdot 17296=103\,776$
  4. medium
    How many sequences of length $6$ with terms $0$ or $1$ have exactly two ones? How many such sequences have at least one one?
    Solution
    You choose the places for the ones (a set of places): $\binom62=15$. There are $2^6=64$ sequences in all, and $1$ without a one. $15$ and $63$
  5. exam-level
    A class has $12$ girls and $10$ boys. In how many ways can we choose a delegation of four that has at least one girl and at least one boy?
    Solution
    All delegations minus all-girl minus all-boy: $\binom{22}{4}-\binom{12}{4}-\binom{10}{4}=7315-495-210$. $6610$
  6. exam-level
    How many six-digit numbers contain two digits $0$, three digits $1$ and one digit $2$?
    Solution
    All arrangements of these digits: $\frac{6!}{2!\,3!\,1!}=60$. Discard those with a zero at the start: the remaining $5$ digits ($0,1,1,1,2$) can be arranged in $\frac{5!}{3!}=20$ ways. $60-20=40$

6. On the exam

  • First decide: sequence or set, and state it in one sentence. Mixing them up (e.g. $\Omega$ as combinations and $A$ as variations) is a classic way to lose points.
  • “Drawn at once” = a set. “One after another without replacement” = variations without repetition. “With replacement” = $n^k$.
  • Elements next to each other: glue them into a block and multiply by the number of arrangements inside the block.
  • The formula for permutations with repetition is not on the sheet: write briefly why you divide by $k_i!$.
  • Compute large binomial coefficients by canceling: $\binom{22}{4}=\frac{22\cdot21\cdot20\cdot19}{24}$, not via factorials.
