Triangles: types, Pythagoras, segments in a right triangle

Whether a triangle is acute, right or obtuse comes down to one comparison: the square of the longest side versus the sum of the squares of the other two. And the altitude from the right angle cuts the triangle into two smaller ones similar to the whole. All the "segment" formulas come from this.

VIII.1, VIII.2, VIII.12

1. Types of triangles and Pythagoras

Notation as on the formula sheet: side $a$ lies opposite angle $\alpha$ (at $A$), $b$ opposite $\beta$, $c$ opposite $\gamma$.

Pythagorean theorem and its converse on CKE sheet $$\gamma=90^\circ\iff a^2+b^2=c^2$$ The arrow "$\Leftarrow$" is the converse: from the sides alone you know the angle is right. Proof: the law of cosines (for $\gamma=90^\circ$ the term $-2ab\cos\gamma$ vanishes) or below, from similarity.
Type of triangle from its sides ($c$ – longest)
$$c^2<a^2+b^2\ \Rightarrow\ \text{acute}$$ $$c^2=a^2+b^2\ \Rightarrow\ \text{right}$$ $$c^2>a^2+b^2\ \Rightarrow\ \text{obtuse}$$
why
$c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma$: the sign of $\cos\gamma$ decides whether $c^2$ is smaller or larger than $a^2+b^2$
$c$
must be the longest: only opposite it can a right or obtuse angle lie
Sides and angles In a triangle the longer side lies opposite the larger angle (and vice versa). Also the triangle inequality: $c<a+b$ for every side.
Short justification

From the law of sines, $a=2R\sin\alpha$, $b=2R\sin\beta$. When $\alpha>\beta$ and $\alpha+\beta<180^\circ$:

$$\sin\alpha-\sin\beta=2\cos\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\sin\tfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}$$

Both factors are positive ($\frac{\alpha+\beta}2<90^\circ$), so $a>b$. $\blacksquare$

Chord (Pythagoras in a circle, VIII.1)
$$\left(\tfrac{l}{2}\right)^2+d^2=r^2$$
$l$
length of the chord; $d$ – its distance from the center. A radius perpendicular to a chord bisects it. This is not on the formula sheet; you derive it from the diagram

Special triangles (equilateral: $h=\frac{a\sqrt3}{2}$, $P=\frac{a^2\sqrt3}{4}$, $r=\frac13h$, $R=\frac23h$; right: $R=\frac c2$, $r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}$) – all on the formula sheet. The $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ and $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ triangles: trigonometry in a right triangle.

3. Segments in a right triangle

Theorem on segments in a right triangle If $CD$ is the altitude of the right triangle $ABC$ with right angle $ACB$, then $$|AD|\cdot|BD|=|CD|^2$$ $$|AC|^2=|AB|\cdot|AD|$$ $$|BC|^2=|AB|\cdot|BD|$$ The formula sheet has only $h_c=\sqrt{|AD|\cdot|DB|}$ and $h_c=\frac{ab}{c}$. Memorize the other two: "leg$^2$ = hypotenuse · its projection".
Proof (required on the exam)

Let $|\angle BAC|=\alpha$, $|\angle ABC|=\beta$, $\alpha+\beta=90^\circ$.

  1. $\triangle ADC\sim\triangle ACB$ (AA): both have a right angle (at $D$ and at $C$) and the common angle $\alpha$. The vertices correspond $A\to A$, $D\to C$, $C\to B$, so $\frac{|AD|}{|AC|}=\frac{|AC|}{|AB|}$, i.e. $|AC|^2=|AB|\cdot|AD|$.
  2. $\triangle CDB\sim\triangle ACB$: a right angle and the common angle $\beta$. $D\to C$, $B\to B$, $C\to A$, so $\frac{|BD|}{|BC|}=\frac{|BC|}{|AB|}$, i.e. $|BC|^2=|AB|\cdot|BD|$.
  3. Hence $\triangle ADC\sim\triangle CDB$; the angle $\alpha$ at $A$ corresponds to the angle $DCB$ (since $|\angle DCB|=90^\circ-\beta=\alpha$). $A\to C$, $D\to D$, $C\to B$, so $\frac{|AD|}{|CD|}=\frac{|CD|}{|BD|}$, i.e. $|AD|\cdot|BD|=|CD|^2$. $\blacksquare$

Bonus: adding the formulas from steps 1 and 2: $|AC|^2+|BC|^2=|AB|(|AD|+|BD|)=|AB|^2$. This is a proof of the Pythagorean theorem.

Drag $C$ along the semicircle (angle $C$ is always right – this is Thales' circle) and drag $A$ and $B$. Mark the equal angles: the angle $\alpha$ at $A$ reappears at $C$ in the purple triangle, $\beta$ in the blue one. That is why the three triangles are similar.

4. Worked example

The altitude $CD$ of the right triangle $ABC$ ($|\angle C|=90^\circ$) divides the hypotenuse into segments $|AD|=4$ and $|BD|=9$. Find $|CD|$, $|AC|$, $|BC|$.

  1. $|CD|^2=|AD|\cdot|BD|=36$, so $|CD|=6$.
  2. $|AB|=13$, $|AC|^2=13\cdot4=52$, so $|AC|=2\sqrt{13}$.
  3. $|BC|^2=13\cdot9=117$, so $|BC|=3\sqrt{13}$.
  4. Check: $52+117=169=13^2$.

$|CD|=6$, $|AC|=2\sqrt{13}$, $|BC|=3\sqrt{13}$

5. Exercises

  1. easy
    A triangle has sides $6$, $8$, $11$. Is it acute, right or obtuse? Which side is opposite the largest angle?
    Solution
    Longest side $11$: $11^2=121$, $6^2+8^2=100<121$. The largest angle lies opposite the side $11$. Obtuse, the obtuse angle is opposite the side $11$.
  2. easy
    In a circle of radius $10$ a chord of length $16$ is drawn. Find the distance of the chord from the center of the circle.
    Solution
    Half the chord is $8$, the radius $10$ is the hypotenuse: $d=\sqrt{100-64}$. $d=6$
  3. medium
    The legs of a triangle have lengths $15$ and $20$. Find the altitude to the hypotenuse and the lengths of the segments into which this altitude divides the hypotenuse.
    Solution
    $c=25$, $h=\frac{ab}{c}=\frac{300}{25}=12$. Projection of the leg $15$: $\frac{15^2}{25}=9$, of the leg $20$: $\frac{400}{25}=16$ (check: $9\cdot16=144=12^2$). $h=12$, segments $9$ and $16$.
  4. medium
    For which $x$ are the numbers $x$, $x+1$, $x+2$ the side lengths of an obtuse triangle?
    Solution
    The triangle exists when $x+(x+1)>x+2$, i.e. $x>1$. The longest side is $x+2$; it is obtuse when $(x+2)^2>x^2+(x+1)^2$, i.e. $x^2-2x-3<0$, $(x-3)(x+1)<0$, $x\in(-1,3)$. Intersection: $x\in(1,3)$. (For $x=3$ you get the triangle $3,4,5$ – right.)
  5. exam-level
    The altitude $CD$ of the right triangle $ABC$ with the right angle at $C$ has length $6$ and divides the hypotenuse in the ratio $|AD|:|DB|=1:4$. Find the area of the triangle.
    Solution
    $|AD|=t$, $|DB|=4t$. $t\cdot4t=36$, so $t=3$, $|AB|=5t=15$. $P=\frac12\cdot15\cdot6$. $P=45$
  6. exam-level
    Prove that if $a$, $b$ are the legs and $h$ the altitude to the hypotenuse, then $\frac1{h^2}=\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}$.
    Solution
    From the two area formulas: $\frac12ab=\frac12ch$, so $h=\frac{ab}{c}$. Hence $$\frac1{h^2}=\frac{c^2}{a^2b^2}=\frac{a^2+b^2}{a^2b^2}$$ (Pythagoras), and this is $\frac1{b^2}+\frac1{a^2}$. Q.E.D.

6. On the exam

  • Type of triangle: compare the square of the longest side with the sum of the squares of the others. Check the triangle inequality first.
  • When citing the converse of Pythagoras, say so explicitly: "$a^2+b^2=c^2$, so by the converse of the Pythagorean theorem the angle $\gamma$ is right".
  • Altitude from the right angle: $h=\frac{ab}{c}$ (from areas) is the fastest way. Formulas with the segments $|AD|$, $|BD|$ only make sense for a right angle.
  • Chord, tangent segment: always draw the radius to the point (of tangency or the chord's midpoint) – this creates a right triangle.
