Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

"We know that $B$ happened" means: throw away everything outside $B$, and $B$ becomes the new, whole world. $P(A|B)$ is the fraction of this new world that $A$ occupies. Bayes' theorem lets you reverse the question: from $P(\text{result}|\text{cause})$ get $P(\text{cause}|\text{result})$.

XII.R.1

1. Definition

Definition For $P(B)>0$ the probability that $A$ occurs given that $B$ occurred: $$P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$$
Consequences
$$P(A\cap B)=P(B)\cdot P(A|B)$$ $$\text{classical model: } P(A|B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}$$
multiplication
this is the "along the branch of the tree" rule: the probability of the first stage times the conditional probability of the second
classical
in the classical model you simply count within the set $B$ instead of $\Omega$
$P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$ are usually different numbers. Confusing them is the most common mistake in this topic (see the medical test below).

Independence (not listed explicitly in the requirements, but it appears in tasks): $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\cdot P(B)$, i.e. $P(A|B)=P(A)$: knowing $B$ changes nothing.

2. Interactive: conditioning narrows the world

The square is $\Omega$ (area $1$). The green strip is $B$, the blue parts are $A$. Click "Condition on B": the rest disappears and $B$ stretches to the whole square. The blue area that remains is exactly $P(A|B)$.

3. Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem
$$P(B|A)=\frac{P(A|B)\cdot P(B)}{P(A)}$$
$P(A)$
you usually compute it from the law of total probability: $P(A)=P(A|B)P(B)+P(A|B')P(B')$
Where does it come from? (one line)
From the definition, write $P(A\cap B)$ in two ways: $P(B|A)\,P(A)=P(A\cap B)=P(A|B)\,P(B)$. Divide by $P(A)$. You do not have to memorize it, just be able to derive it.

On the tree: $P(B|A)=\dfrac{\text{branch through }B\text{ to }A}{\text{all branches to }A}$.

The test has sensitivity $P(+|\text{sick})$ and specificity $P(-|\text{healthy})$. The thickness of a branch is the probability of the whole path. Set the disease frequency to $1\%$: despite a "good" test most $+$ results are healthy people (bottom bar).

5. Worked example

We roll a die twice. It is known that the sum of the dots is at least $10$. Find the probability that the first roll was a six.

  1. $B$ (sum $\ge10$): $(4,6),(5,5),(6,4),(5,6),(6,5),(6,6)$, $|B|=6$.
  2. $A\cap B$ (first a six and sum $\ge10$): $(6,4),(6,5),(6,6)$, $|A\cap B|=3$.
  3. $P(A|B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}=\frac36$.

$P(A|B)=\frac12$ (without the condition it would be $\frac16$)

Machine $M_1$ makes $60\%$ of the products, of which $2\%$ are defective; machine $M_2$ makes $40\%$, of which $5\%$ are defective. A randomly chosen product is defective. What is the probability that it was made by $M_1$?

  1. Branches to "defective": $0.6\cdot0.02=0.012$ and $0.4\cdot0.05=0.02$.
  2. $P(W)=0.012+0.02=0.032$.
  3. $P(M_1|W)=\frac{0.012}{0.032}=\frac{12}{32}$.

$P(M_1|W)=\frac38$

6. Exercises

  1. easy
    $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.4$, $P(A\cap B)=0.2$. Find $P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$.
    Solution
    $P(A|B)=\frac{0.2}{0.4}$, $P(B|A)=\frac{0.2}{0.5}$. $P(A|B)=\frac12$, $P(B|A)=\frac25$
  2. easy
    We roll a die twice. The sum of the dots is even. Find the probability that a double was rolled (the same number twice).
    Solution
    The sum is even when both results are even or both odd: $|B|=9+9=18$. Every double has an even sum, so $|A\cap B|=6$. $P(A|B)=\frac{6}{18}=\frac13$
  3. medium
    A class has $18$ girls and $12$ boys. $10$ girls and $4$ boys wear glasses. We pick a person wearing glasses. Find the probability that it is a girl.
    Solution
    The condition "glasses" narrows the world to $14$ people, of whom $10$ are girls. $P=\frac{10}{14}=\frac57$
  4. medium
    $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B|A)=0.5$, $P(A\cup B)=0.8$. Find $P(B)$ and $P(A|B)$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent?
    Solution
    $P(A\cap B)=0.6\cdot0.5=0.3$. $P(B)=P(A\cup B)-P(A)+P(A\cap B)=0.8-0.6+0.3=0.5$. $P(A|B)=\frac{0.3}{0.5}=\frac35$. Since $P(A)P(B)=0.3=P(A\cap B)$, the events are independent. $P(B)=\frac12$, $P(A|B)=\frac35$, independent
  5. exam-level
    $10\%$ of the population has the disease. The test is positive for $90\%$ of the sick and for $10\%$ of the healthy. A randomly chosen person tests positive. Find the probability that the person is sick.
    Solution
    $P(+)=0.1\cdot0.9+0.9\cdot0.1=0.18$. $P(C|+)=\frac{0.09}{0.18}$. The test is "accurate in $90\%$", yet a positive result gives only fifty-fifty. $P(C|+)=\frac12$
  6. exam-level
    Urn I: $3$ white and $2$ black balls, urn II: $1$ white and $4$ black. We toss a coin: heads → we draw a ball from urn I, tails → from urn II. A white ball was drawn. Find the probability that it came from urn I.
    Solution
    $P(B)=\frac12\cdot\frac35+\frac12\cdot\frac15=\frac{4}{10}=\frac25$. $P(I|B)=\frac{\frac12\cdot\frac35}{\frac25}=\frac{3/10}{4/10}$. $P(I|B)=\frac34$

7. On the exam

  • Name the events in words ("$A$: a white ball was drawn", "$B$: …") and write which probability you are computing: $P(A|B)$ or $P(B|A)$.
  • In the classical model compute $\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}$: usually faster than via $P(A\cap B)$ and $P(B)$.
  • Bayes = tree: draw the tree, the result is "my branch to $A$" over "all branches to $A$". A tree with labeled branches and numbers is a full-fledged solution.
  • Check that the result makes sense: probability $\in[0,1]$, fraction in lowest terms.
