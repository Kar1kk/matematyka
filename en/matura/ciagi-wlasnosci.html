Sequences: general term, recursion, monotonicity

A sequence is a function whose arguments are only the natural numbers $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ Its graph is separate points $(n, a_n)$, and you read monotonicity from the sign of the "jump" $a_{n+1}-a_n$.

VI.1–VI.3

1. Definitions

A numerical sequence is a function $a\colon \N_+\to\R$. Instead of $a(n)$ we write $a_n$ and call it the $n$-th term. A sequence may be finite (domain $\{1,\ldots,k\}$).
General term
$$a_n=\frac{2n-1}{n+1}$$
$a_5$
substitute $n=5$: $a_5=\frac{9}{6}=\frac32$
Recursive formula
$$a_1=2,\quad a_{n+1}=3a_n-2$$
next terms
$a_2=4$, $a_3=10$, $a_4=28$: each term from the previous one
Monotonicity A sequence $(a_n)$ is increasing when $a_{n+1}>a_n$ for every $n$; decreasing when $a_{n+1}<a_n$; constant when $a_{n+1}=a_n$; non-decreasing ($\ge$) and non-increasing ($\le$). Monotonic = one of these types.
How to test monotonicity
$$a_{n+1}-a_n\ \gtrless\ 0\ ?$$
difference
simplify $a_{n+1}-a_n$ to a single fraction and test the sign for all $n\in\N_+$
quotient
only when all $a_n>0$: $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}>1$ ⇒ increasing, $<1$ ⇒ decreasing (handy with powers and factorials)
To show that a sequence is not monotonic, a counterexample is enough: three terms, e.g. $a_1<a_2$ and $a_2>a_3$. On the other hand, "the first terms increase" proves nothing.

Each next term is computed from the previous one. Click "Step" and watch the point "jump" to the next $n$. Change $a_1$: for $a_{n+1}=2a_n-3$ and $a_1=3$ the sequence is constant, for $a_1>3$ it increases, for $a_1<3$ it decreases.

4. Worked example

Investigate the monotonicity of the sequence $a_n=\frac{2n-1}{n+1}$.

  1. $a_{n+1}=\frac{2(n+1)-1}{(n+1)+1}=\frac{2n+1}{n+2}$.
  2. $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{(2n+1)(n+1)-(2n-1)(n+2)}{(n+1)(n+2)}$.
  3. Numerator: $(2n^2+3n+1)-(2n^2+3n-2)=3$.
  4. $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{3}{(n+1)(n+2)}>0$ for every $n\in\N_+$ (the denominator is positive).

The sequence is increasing.

Is the sequence $a_n=n^2-9n$ monotonic? Which term is the smallest?

  1. $a_{n+1}-a_n=(n+1)^2-9(n+1)-n^2+9n=2n-8$.
  2. $2n-8<0$ for $n<4$, $=0$ for $n=4$, $>0$ for $n>4$: the sequence first decreases, then increases.
  3. The smallest are $a_4=a_5=-20$ (for $n=4$ the difference is $0$).

Not monotonic (e.g. $a_1>a_2$, $a_5<a_6$); the smallest terms: $a_4=a_5=-20$.

5. Exercises

  1. easy
    A sequence is given by the formula $a_n=n^2-7n+10$. Which terms of this sequence are negative?
    Solution
    $n^2-7n+10=(n-2)(n-5)<0 \iff 2<n<5$, and $n\in\N_+$, so $n\in\{3,4\}$. The negative ones are $a_3=a_4=-2$.
  2. easy
    A sequence is defined recursively: $a_1=2$, $a_{n+1}=3a_n-2$. Find $a_4$.
    Solution
    $a_2=3\cdot2-2=4$, $a_3=3\cdot4-2=10$, $a_4=3\cdot10-2=28$. $a_4=28$
  3. medium
    Investigate the monotonicity of the sequence $a_n=\frac{3n+1}{n+2}$.
    Solution
    $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{3n+4}{n+3}-\frac{3n+1}{n+2}=\frac{(3n+4)(n+2)-(3n+1)(n+3)}{(n+2)(n+3)}$. Numerator: $3n^2+10n+8-(3n^2+10n+3)=5$. Hence $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{5}{(n+2)(n+3)}>0$. The sequence is increasing.
  4. medium
    Investigate the monotonicity of the sequence $a_n=\frac{2^n}{n!}$.
    Solution
    All terms are positive, so use the quotient: $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{2^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\cdot\frac{n!}{2^n}=\frac{2}{n+1}$. For $n=1$ the quotient is $1$ ($a_1=a_2=2$), for $n\ge2$ it is $<1$. The sequence is non-increasing, but not decreasing (since $a_1=a_2$).
  5. exam-level
    Find all values of the parameter $k$ for which the sequence $a_n=\frac{kn-1}{n+1}$ is increasing.
    Solution
    Separate the integer part: $a_n=\frac{k(n+1)-k-1}{n+1}=k-\frac{k+1}{n+1}$. Hence $a_{n+1}-a_n=(k+1)\left(\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+2}\right)=\frac{k+1}{(n+1)(n+2)}$. The denominator is positive, so the difference is positive for every $n$ if and only if $k+1>0$. $k>-1$

6. On the exam

  • Pattern: find $a_{n+1}$ (substitute $n+1$ in parentheses), compute $a_{n+1}-a_n$, bring to a common denominator, justify the sign "for every $n\in\N_+$". Without that sentence you lose a point.
  • The quotient only for sequences with positive terms. You compare with $1$, not with $0$.
  • A quadratic sequence $a_n=an^2+bn+c$: the vertex $p$ need not be a natural number. The smallest/largest term is at the natural number closest to $p$ (check both neighbors).
  • "Which terms are…": you solve an inequality, but the answer is only $n\in\N_+$. A common mistake: giving an interval instead of the indices.
