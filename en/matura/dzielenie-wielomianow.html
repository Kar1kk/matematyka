Dividing a polynomial by x − a, Horner's scheme

Every polynomial can be written as $(x-a)\cdot Q(x)+r$, where the remainder $r$ is a number. Substitute $x=a$: the bracket vanishes and $r=W(a)$ remains. Horner's scheme computes $Q$ and $r$ in one sweep: "multiply by $a$, add".

II.R.1

1. Theorems

Theorem (division with remainder by $x-a$) For a polynomial $W$ of degree $n\ge1$ and a number $a$ there is exactly one polynomial $Q$ of degree $n-1$ and exactly one number $r$ such that $$W(x)=(x-a)\cdot Q(x)+r$$ The coefficients of $Q$ and the remainder are given by Horner's formulas: for $W(x)=w_nx^n+\ldots+w_0$ and $Q(x)=q_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+q_0$ $$q_{n-1}=w_n,\qquad q_{k-1}=w_k+a\,q_k$$ $$r=w_0+a\,q_0$$
Proof (required on the exam, for $n=4$)

Let $W(x)=w_4x^4+w_3x^3+w_2x^2+w_1x+w_0$, $w_4\ne0$. We look for $Q(x)=q_3x^3+q_2x^2+q_1x+q_0$ and a number $r$. Multiply out:

$$\begin{aligned}(x-a)Q(x)+r=\;&q_3x^4+(q_2-aq_3)x^3\\&+(q_1-aq_2)x^2\\&+(q_0-aq_1)x\\&+(r-aq_0)\end{aligned}$$

Polynomials are equal if and only if their coefficients are equal (equality of polynomials), so:

$$\begin{aligned}q_3&=w_4\\ q_2-aq_3&=w_3\\ q_1-aq_2&=w_2\\ q_0-aq_1&=w_1\\ r-aq_0&=w_0\end{aligned}$$

From successive equations we find $q_3=w_4$, $q_2=w_3+aq_3$, $q_1=w_2+aq_2$, $q_0=w_1+aq_1$, $r=w_0+aq_0$. So the system has exactly one solution: such $Q$ and $r$ exist (the formulas give them directly) and are unique (any other would have to satisfy the same system). Since $q_3=w_4\ne0$, $Q$ has degree $3=n-1$. These are Horner's formulas. For any $n$ the computation is identical. $\blacksquare$

Remainder theorem The remainder of dividing $W(x)$ by $x-a$ equals $W(a)$.
Proof

Substitute $x=a$ into $W(x)=(x-a)Q(x)+r$: $\;W(a)=0\cdot Q(a)+r=r$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary (Bézout's theorem) $W(x)$ is divisible by $x-a$ $\iff$ $W(a)=0$, i.e. when $a$ is a root. Then $W(x)=(x-a)\cdot Q(x)$.
These theorems are not on the formula sheet. You have to remember them and be able to prove them.

2. Horner's scheme

Write all the coefficients of $W$ in a row, including zeros for missing powers. Copy the first one down, then repeat: multiply by $a$, add to the next one. The last number is the remainder, the others are the coefficients of $Q$ (degree $1$ lower). Dividing by $x+3$ is dividing by $x-(-3)$, i.e. $a=-3$.

You can also divide "by long division", like numbers. Horner is the same computation, only without rewriting the powers of $x$.

5. Step-by-step example

Divide $W(x)=2x^4-3x^3+5x-6$ by $x-2$.

  1. Coefficients with a zero for $x^2$: $2,\,-3,\,0,\,5,\,-6$; $\;a=2$.
  2. The scheme:
    $2$$-3$$0$$5$$-6$
    $a=2$$2$$1$$2$$9$$12$
    e.g. $1=-3+2\cdot2$, $\;2=0+2\cdot1$, $\;9=5+2\cdot2$, $\;12=-6+2\cdot9$.
  3. Check with the remainder theorem: $W(2)=32-24+10-6=12$. It agrees.

$W(x)=(x-2)(2x^3+x^2+2x+9)+12$

6. Tasks

  1. easy
    Divide $x^3-4x^2+x+6$ by $x-2$ using Horner's scheme.
    Solution
    Row: $1,\,-2,\,-3,\,0$. $Q(x)=x^2-2x-3$, $r=0$, so $x-2$ divides this polynomial.
  2. easy
    Find the remainder of dividing $W(x)=x^5-3x^3+2x-1$ by $x+1$.
    Solution
    $r=W(-1)=-1+3-2-1$. $r=-1$
  3. medium
    For what $m$ is the polynomial $W(x)=x^3+mx^2-5x+6$ divisible by $x-3$?
    Solution
    Bézout: $W(3)=27+9m-15+6=18+9m=0$. $m=-2$
  4. medium
    The polynomial $W(x)=x^3+ax^2+bx-6$ is divisible by $x-1$, and dividing by $x+1$ leaves remainder $-8$. Find $a$ and $b$.
    Solution
    $W(1)=a+b-5=0$, $\;W(-1)=a-b-7=-8$. Hence $a+b=5$, $a-b=-1$. $a=2$, $b=3$
  5. exam-level
    It is known that $W(1)=3$ and $W(-2)=-3$. The polynomial was written as $W(x)=(x-1)(x+2)\cdot P(x)+px+q$. Find $p$ and $q$ (i.e. the remainder of dividing $W$ by $(x-1)(x+2)$).
    Solution
    Substitute $x=1$ and $x=-2$, the product vanishes: $p+q=3$, $\;-2p+q=-3$. Subtracting: $3p=6$. $p=2$, $q=1$, remainder $2x+1$
  6. exam-level
    Find $a$ and $b$ for which the number $2$ is a double root of the polynomial $W(x)=x^3+ax+b$.
    Solution
    Horner for the coefficients $1,0,a,b$ and the number $2$: $\;1,\;2,\;a+4,\;b+2a+8$. Remainder $=0$: $b=-2a-8$, quotient $Q(x)=x^2+2x+a+4$.
    $2$ must also be a root of $Q$: $Q(2)=12+a=0$, so $a=-12$, $b=16$. Check: $W(x)=(x-2)^2(x+4)$. $a=-12$, $b=16$

7. On the exam

  • The most common mistake in Horner: a missed zero for a missing power. The second: dividing by $x+3$ with $a=3$ instead of $a=-3$.
  • Asked only for the remainder? Do not divide, compute $W(a)$.
  • Write the result in full form $W(x)=(x-a)Q(x)+r$; the remainder is a number, the quotient has degree $1$ lower.
  • The proof of the division theorem (e.g. for degree $4$) is on the list of required ones: comparing coefficients and the formulas $q_{k-1}=w_k+aq_k$.
