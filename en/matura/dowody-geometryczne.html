Geometric proofs

A geometric proof is a chain of short steps, each with its “because…”. A drawing suggests an idea but proves nothing – the relation must hold for every figure satisfying the assumptions, including the ones you did not draw.

VIII.11 (tools: VIII.4–VIII.10)

1. How to write a proof

  1. A drawing with labels (vertices, angles $\alpha,\beta$, lengths $x$). Introduce the labels in words: “let $|\angle BAC|=\alpha$”.
  2. Assumption – what we know. Claim – what we have to show. Write them in symbols.
  3. Proof: every sentence = a fact + a justification in brackets: “$|AS|=|SC|$ (the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other)”.
  4. Conclusion: return to the claim in words – “which was to be shown”.
You must not start from the claim (“suppose that $|AK|=|BL|$, then…”) or use something that is merely “visible in the drawing” (that an angle is right, that points are collinear).

3. Interactive: always, not only in the drawing

Drag the vertices of any quadrilateral – even a concave one. The midpoints of the sides always form a parallelogram whose sides are parallel to the diagonals and have half their length (Varignon's theorem, proof below).

Drag point $P$ inside an equilateral triangle. The sum of the distances from the sides is constant and equal to the altitude – even though each of them changes. The coloured triangles are the proof “by areas” (example 2).

4. Step-by-step examples

Example 1. Prove that the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral are the vertices of a parallelogram.

Assumption: $K$, $L$, $M$, $N$ are the midpoints of sides $AB$, $BC$, $CD$, $DA$ of quadrilateral $ABCD$. Claim: $KLMN$ is a parallelogram.

  1. In triangle $ABC$ segment $KL$ joins the midpoints of two sides, so $KL\parallel AC$ and $|KL|=\frac12|AC|$ (midsegment of a triangle; proof: converse of Thales' theorem).
  2. Similarly in triangle $ACD$: $NM\parallel AC$ and $|NM|=\frac12|AC|$.
  3. Hence $KL\parallel NM$ and $|KL|=|NM|$. A quadrilateral in which two opposite sides are parallel and equal is a parallelogram.

$KLMN$ is a parallelogram, which was to be shown.

Example 2. Prove that the sum of the distances from any point $P$ inside an equilateral triangle to its sides equals the altitude $h$ of this triangle.

Assumption: $ABC$ is equilateral with side $a$ and altitude $h$, $P$ is inside, $d_1,d_2,d_3$ are the distances from $P$ to $BC$, $CA$, $AB$. Claim: $d_1+d_2+d_3=h$.

  1. Segments $PA$, $PB$, $PC$ divide the triangle into three triangles: $P_{ABC}=P_{PBC}+P_{PCA}+P_{PAB}$.
  2. Each has base $a$ and height $d_i$: $\frac12ah=\frac12ad_1+\frac12ad_2+\frac12ad_3$.
  3. Dividing by $\frac12a>0$: $h=d_1+d_2+d_3$.

$d_1+d_2+d_3=h$, which was to be shown.

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    Prove that the bisectors of two adjacent supplementary angles are perpendicular.
    Solution
    Supplementary adjacent angles: $\alpha+\beta=180^\circ$. The bisectors make angles $\frac\alpha2$ and $\frac\beta2$ with the common arm, so the angle between them is $\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}=90^\circ$. The bisectors are perpendicular.
  2. medium
    The diagonals of parallelogram $ABCD$ meet at point $S$. A line through $S$ meets sides $AB$ and $CD$ at points $K$ and $L$. Prove that $|SK|=|SL|$.
    Solution
    Triangles $ASK$ and $CSL$: $|AS|=|SC|$ (the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other), $|\angle KAS|=|\angle LCS|$ (alternate angles, $AB\parallel CD$), $|\angle ASK|=|\angle CSL|$ (vertical angles). By ASA they are congruent, so $|SK|=|SL|$.
  3. medium
    In triangle $ABC$ the altitudes $AD$ and $BE$ have equal lengths. Prove that $|AC|=|BC|$.
    Solution
    The area in two ways: $\frac12|BC|\cdot|AD|=\frac12|AC|\cdot|BE|$. Since $|AD|=|BE|>0$, we divide by $\frac12|AD|$: $|BC|=|AC|$.
  4. exam-level
    $AD$ and $BE$ are altitudes of acute triangle $ABC$. Prove that triangles $CDE$ and $CAB$ are similar.
    Solution
    Let $|\angle ACB|=\gamma$. In right triangle $ADC$: $|CD|=|CA|\cos\gamma$. In right triangle $BEC$: $|CE|=|CB|\cos\gamma$. Hence $\frac{|CD|}{|CA|}=\frac{|CE|}{|CB|}=\cos\gamma$, and angle $\gamma$ is common and lies between these sides. By SAS similarity $\triangle CDE\sim\triangle CAB$ (with scale $\cos\gamma$).
  5. exam-level
    The diagonals of trapezoid $ABCD$ ($AB\parallel CD$) meet at point $S$. Prove that triangles $ASD$ and $BSC$ have equal areas.
    Solution
    Triangles $ABD$ and $ABC$ have the common base $AB$, and $D$ and $C$ lie on a line parallel to $AB$ – the heights are equal, so $P_{ABD}=P_{ABC}$. Subtract the area of triangle $ABS$ from both: $P_{ASD}=P_{BSC}$.
  6. exam-level
    On sides $AC$ and $BC$ of acute triangle $ABC$, squares $ACDE$ and $BCFG$ are constructed outwards. Prove that $|AF|=|BD|$.
    Solution
    Triangles $ACF$ and $DCB$: $|AC|=|DC|$ (sides of square $ACDE$), $|CF|=|CB|$ (sides of square $BCFG$), $|\angle ACF|=\gamma+90^\circ=|\angle DCB|$. By SAS they are congruent, so $|AF|=|BD|$. (Bonus: $AF\perp BD$, because one triangle is the other rotated by $90^\circ$ about $C$.)

6. On the exam

  • Full marks require a justification of every step. For congruence/similarity give the name of the criterion and all three equalities.
  • Do not assume a special case (isosceles triangle, right angle) unless it is in the assumptions.
  • When the claim involves a ratio or a product of segments – look for similar triangles; for equal areas – a common base or height.
  • A proof “from the claim” is acceptable only as a chain of equivalences ($\iff$) ending in a true statement – it is safer to write from the assumption to the claim.
