Proofs: divisibility and remainders, irrationality

A divisibility proof is almost always the same move: write the number in general form ($2k+1$, $4k+3$, $n(n+1)$…) and factor the divisor out. The irrationality of a number and the infinitude of primes are proved indirectly: assume the opposite and reach a contradiction.

I.2 · proofs: infinitely many primes, irrationality of $\sqrt2$, $\log_2 5$

1. Tools and the write-up template

Division with remainder (memorize)
$$n=mk+r,\quad k\in\Z,\ \ r\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}$$
even
$2k$; odd $2k+1$
consecutive
among $m$ consecutive integers exactly one is divisible by $m$; the product of two consecutive integers is even
remainders
the remainder of a sum/product = the remainder of the sum/product of the remainders, because $(mk+r)(ml+s)=m(\dots)+rs$

Write-up template for the exam

  1. State the assumption as a formula: “Let $n=4k+3$, where $k\in\Z$”.
  2. Transform the expression and factor out the divisor: $\ldots=4(\ldots)+r$.
  3. Name what is in the brackets: “$4k^2+6k+2$ is an integer”.
  4. Conclusion in words: “so the number is divisible by $4$ / leaves remainder $r$”.

2. Two proofs from the core curriculum

Theorem The product of four consecutive natural numbers is divisible by $24$.
Proof

Let $P=n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)$. Among three (and hence among four) consecutive numbers one is divisible by $3$, so $3\mid P$.

Among four consecutive numbers there are two consecutive even numbers $2j$ and $2j+2=2(j+1)$. Their product is $4j(j+1)$, and $j(j+1)$ is even, so $8\mid P$.

$3$ and $8$ are relatively prime, so $24=3\cdot8\mid P$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem A number that leaves remainder $3$ when divided by $4$ is not the square of an integer.
Proof

We check which remainders squares leave when divided by $4$. Every integer is $2j$ or $2j+1$:

$$(2j)^2=4j^2$$ $$(2j+1)^2=4(j^2+j)+1$$

So a square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, never $3$. $\blacksquare$

3. Interactive: the remainder clock

The “clock” has $m$ remainders. The blue point is the remainder of $n$, the red one the remainder of $n^k$. “▶ Play” goes through consecutive $n$: only the remainders that the powers actually reach light up. For $k=2$, $m=4$ only $0$ and $1$ remain — that is the whole proof from section 2.

4. Indirect proofs

Template: assume the negation of the claim, derive something false from it, conclude that the assumption was false.

Theorem $\sqrt2$ is an irrational number.
Proof (required on the exam)

Suppose $\sqrt2=\frac pq$, where $p,q$ are positive integers and the fraction is in lowest terms. Then $p^2=2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, and therefore $p$ is even (the square of an odd number is odd). Let $p=2s$:

$$4s^2=2q^2\ \Rightarrow\ q^2=2s^2$$

In the same way $q$ is even. But then $\frac pq$ can be reduced by $2$ — a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Theorem $\log_2 5$ is an irrational number.
Proof (required on the exam)

$\log_2 5>0$, because $5>1$. Suppose $\log_2 5=\frac pq$, where $p,q$ are positive integers. By the definition of the logarithm:

$$2^{p/q}=5\ \Rightarrow\ 2^p=5^q$$

The left side is even ($p\ge1$), the right side is odd — a contradiction. $\blacksquare$ The irrationality of e.g. $\log_3 2$ or $\log 5$ is proved the same way (then $10^p=5^q$: the left side is even).

Theorem (Euclid) There are infinitely many primes.
Proof (required on the exam)

Suppose there are finitely many: $p_1,p_2,\dots,p_k$. Let

$$N=p_1p_2\cdots p_k+1$$

$N>1$, so it has a prime divisor $p$. By assumption $p$ is one of the $p_i$. But $p_i$ divides the product $p_1\cdots p_k$, so $N$ leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $p_i$ — it is not divisible by $p_i$. A contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Take the first $k$ primes and build $N$. “▶ Play” checks them one by one: every $p_i$ leaves remainder $1$. So the factorization of $N$ contains a prime from outside the list. Note: for $k=6$, $N=30031=59\cdot509$ is not prime — the proof does not require it to be.

5. Step-by-step example

Show that for every integer $n$ the number $n^3-n$ is divisible by $6$.

  1. Factor: $n^3-n=n(n^2-1)=(n-1)n(n+1)$ — a product of three consecutive integers.
  2. Among them there is an even number, so $2\mid n^3-n$; there is also a number divisible by $3$, so $3\mid n^3-n$.
  3. $2$ and $3$ are relatively prime.

$6\mid n^3-n$ for every $n\in\Z$

6. Tasks

  1. easy
    Show that the sum of three consecutive integers is divisible by $3$.
    Solution
    $(n-1)+n+(n+1)=3n$, $n\in\Z$. $3\mid 3n$
  2. easy
    Show that the square of an odd number leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $8$.
    Solution
    $(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=4k(k+1)+1$. $k(k+1)$ is even, $k(k+1)=2j$, so $(2k+1)^2=8j+1$. Remainder $1$
  3. medium
    An integer $n$ leaves remainder $2$ when divided by $5$. What remainder does $n^3+2n$ leave?
    Solution
    $n=5k+2$: $(5k+2)^3+2(5k+2)=125k^3+150k^2+70k+12$ $$=5(25k^3+30k^2+14k+2)+2$$ Remainder $2$ (faster: remainders $2^3+2\cdot2=12$, and $12$ leaves remainder $2$).
  4. medium
    Show that a number leaving remainder $3$ when divided by $4$ is not the sum of the squares of two integers.
    Solution
    Squares leave remainders $0$ or $1$ (section 2). The sum of two such remainders is $0$, $1$ or $2$. Remainder $3$ is impossible.
  5. exam-level
    Show that for every integer $n$ the number $n^5-n$ is divisible by $30$.
    Solution
    $n^5-n=n(n^2-1)(n^2+1)$. Write $n^2+1=(n^2-4)+5$: $$n^5-n=n(n^2-1)(n^2-4)+5n(n^2-1)$$ $$=(n-2)(n-1)n(n+1)(n+2)$$ $$+\,5(n-1)n(n+1)$$ The first term is a product of $5$ consecutive numbers: it is divisible by $2$, $3$ and $5$. The second: $5\cdot$(a product of $3$ consecutive numbers), and that is divisible by $6$. Both terms are divisible by $30$, so the sum is too.
  6. exam-level
    Show that $\log_3 2$ is an irrational number.
    Solution
    $\log_3 2>0$. Suppose $\log_3 2=\frac pq$, $p,q\in\N_+$. Then $3^{p/q}=2$, that is $3^p=2^q$. The left side is odd, the right side even ($q\ge1$) — a contradiction. $\log_3 2\notin\Q$

7. On the exam

  • Always “$k\in\Z$” (or $\N$) when you write $2k+1$, $4k+3$. Without it the proof is incomplete.
  • Examples are not a proof. Checking $n=1,2,3$ is good for getting a feel, but earns no points.
  • Combining divisors: from $3\mid P$ and $8\mid P$ it follows that $24\mid P$, because $3$ and $8$ are relatively prime. From $4\mid P$ and $6\mid P$ it does not follow that $24\mid P$ (e.g. $12$).
  • Indirect proof: write explicitly “Suppose that…”, point out the contradiction and finish with a conclusion.
  • A remainder satisfies $0\le r<m$: $-1$ divided by $4$ has remainder $3$.
