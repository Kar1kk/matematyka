The three perpendiculars theorem

You test perpendicularity to a slanted line on its “shadow”: a line $m$ in a plane is perpendicular to a slanted line $k$ exactly when it is perpendicular to the projection $l$ of that line. This lets you see right angles in solids on the projection.

X.R.1

1. Two theorems

Theorem on a line perpendicular to a plane Lines $k$, $l$ and $m$ lie in one plane. If lines $k$ and $l$ have exactly one common point and a line $n$ is perpendicular to both of them, then the line $n$ is also perpendicular to the line $m$.
Proof (required on the exam)

Translating the lines (this does not change angles), we may assume that $n$ and $m$ pass through the point $O$ common to $k$ and $l$. If $m=k$ or $m=l$, the claim is obvious.

  1. On $n$ take points $P$, $P'$ symmetric with respect to $O$: $|OP|=|OP'|$.
  2. In the plane draw a line not through $O$ that meets $k$, $l$, $m$ at points $K$, $L$, $M$.
  3. $k$ is perpendicular to $PP'$ at its midpoint, so $|KP|=|KP'|$. Likewise $|LP|=|LP'|$.
  4. Triangles $KLP$ and $KLP'$ are congruent (SSS), so $\sphericalangle PKL=\sphericalangle P'KL$.
  5. Triangles $PKM$ and $P'KM$ are congruent (SAS: $|KP|=|KP'|$, common side $KM$, equal angles at $K$), so $|MP|=|MP'|$.
  6. Triangle $PMP'$ is isosceles and $O$ is the midpoint of its base, so $MO\perp PP'$, that is $m\perp n$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary: a line perpendicular to two intersecting lines of a plane is perpendicular to the whole plane.

The three perpendiculars theorem A line $k$ meets a plane $P$ and is not perpendicular to it. The line $l$ is the orthogonal projection of the line $k$ onto the plane $P$. The line $m$ lies in the plane $P$. Then the lines $k$ and $m$ are perpendicular if and only if the lines $l$ and $m$ are perpendicular. on CKE sheet
Proof (required on the exam)

Take a point $A\in k$ outside $P$ and its projection $A'\in l$. The line $AA'$ is perpendicular to $P$, so $AA'\perp m$. The lines $k$, $l$, $AA'$ lie in one plane $Q$ (the projecting plane), and $AA'$ meets $k$ at $A$ and $l$ at $A'$.

  • ($\Leftarrow$) $m\perp l$ and $m\perp AA'$, and $l$, $AA'$ intersect at $A'$. By the previous theorem (in the plane $Q$) $m\perp k$.
  • ($\Rightarrow$) $m\perp k$ and $m\perp AA'$, and $k$, $AA'$ intersect at $A$. By the previous theorem $m\perp l$. $\blacksquare$

The assumption “$k$ is not perpendicular to $P$” guarantees that the projection $l$ is a line and not a point.

The theorem is about the angle between $k$ and $m$, not about an intersection point: $m$ need not pass through the point where $k$ pierces the plane (then $k$ and $m$ are skew).

3. Worked example

The edge $DA$ of a tetrahedron $ABCD$ is perpendicular to the plane $ABC$, and $\sphericalangle ABC=90^\circ$. Given: $|AB|=3$, $|BC|=4$, $|DA|=4$. Find the area of the face $BCD$.

  1. The projection of the line $DB$ onto the plane $ABC$ is $AB$ (because $D$ projects to $A$).
  2. $BC\perp AB$, so by the three perpendiculars theorem $BC\perp DB$: the triangle $BCD$ has a right angle at $B$.
  3. $|DB|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$, so $P=\frac12\cdot 5\cdot 4$.

$P_{BCD}=10$

4. Tasks

  1. easy
    Justify that in a cube $ABCDEFGH$ the diagonal $AG$ is perpendicular to the base diagonal $BD$.
    Solution
    The projection of $AG$ onto the plane $ABCD$ is $AC$ ($G$ projects to $C$). The diagonals of a square are perpendicular: $AC\perp BD$. By the three perpendiculars theorem $AG\perp BD$.
  2. medium
    The segment $SA$ of length $4$ is perpendicular to the plane of a square $ABCD$ with side $3$. Find the distance from the point $S$ to the line $BC$.
    Solution
    The projection of $SB$ is $AB\perp BC$, so $SB\perp BC$ and the distance is $|SB|=\sqrt{16+9}$. $5$
  3. medium
    A regular square pyramid has base edge $6$ and height $4$. Justify that the segment $SE$ ($E$ – the midpoint of edge $BC$) is the altitude of the face $BCS$, and find the lateral surface area.
    Solution
    The foot of the height $O$ is the center of the square, $OE\perp BC$, and $OE$ is the projection of $SE$. By the three perpendiculars theorem $SE\perp BC$. $|SE|=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5$, $P_b=4\cdot\frac12\cdot 6\cdot 5$. $P_b=60$
  4. exam-level
    In triangle $ABC$: $|AB|=|AC|=10$, $|BC|=12$. The segment $DA$ of length $6$ is perpendicular to the plane $ABC$. Find the area of triangle $BCD$ and the tangent of the angle between the planes $BCD$ and $ABC$.
    Solution
    $M$ – the midpoint of $BC$; $AM\perp BC$, $|AM|=\sqrt{100-36}=8$. $AM$ is the projection of $DM$, so $DM\perp BC$ and $|DM|=\sqrt{64+36}=10$. The area is $\frac12\cdot12\cdot10=60$. The angle between the planes is $\sphericalangle DMA$: $\tan=\frac{6}{8}$. $P=60$, $\tan\alpha=\frac34$
  5. exam-level
    In a tetrahedron $ABCD$: $AB\perp CD$ and $AC\perp BD$. Show that $AD\perp BC$.
    Solution
    Let $H$ be the projection of $A$ onto the plane $BCD$. The projection of $AB$ is $BH$, and $CD$ lies in $BCD$, so from $AB\perp CD$ (three perpendiculars theorem) $BH\perp CD$. Likewise $CH\perp BD$. Hence $H$ lies on two altitudes of the triangle $BCD$, so it is its orthocenter, and $DH\perp BC$. Again by the three perpendiculars theorem (the projection of $AD$ is $DH$) $AD\perp BC$. (If $H=D$, then $AD$ is perpendicular to the plane $BCD$, hence to $BC$ as well.)

5. On the exam

  • When you cite the theorem, name all three lines: which one is the slanted line, which segment is its projection, which line lies in the plane.
  • Typical uses: the altitude of a lateral face of a pyramid, the distance from a point to a line, a dihedral angle (both arms perpendicular to the edge).
  • The projection determines the foot of the perpendicular: first justify where the foot of the height lies (e.g. the center of the circumscribed circle).
  • In proofs, “$n\perp$ plane” requires perpendicularity to two intersecting lines of that plane.
