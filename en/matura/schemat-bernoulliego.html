Bernoulli trials

You repeat the same independent experiment with two outcomes $n$ times: success (with probability $p$) or failure. One specific order of $k$ successes has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, and there are $\binom nk$ such orders.

XII.R.2, XI.R.2

1. The formula

Bernoulli trials: $n$ independent trials, in each a success with the same probability $p$, a failure with $q=1-p$. $S_n$ is the number of successes.
Probability of $k$ successes on CKE sheet
$$P(S_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$
$\binom nk$
in how many ways to choose which $k$ of the $n$ trials are successes
$p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$
the probability of one fixed order (independence → multiplication)
Derivation: why $\binom nk$?

Write the result of $n$ trials as a string of letters S (success) and F (failure), e.g. for $n=4$, $k=2$: SSFF, SFSF, SFFS, FSSF, FSFS, FFSS. Each such string has probability $p\cdot p\cdot q\cdot q=p^2q^2$ (the trials are independent, the order of the factors does not matter).

The number of strings with exactly $k$ letters S equals the number of $k$-element subsets of the positions $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, i.e. $\binom nk$ (here $\binom42=6$). The strings are disjoint events, so their probabilities add up: $\binom nk p^kq^{n-k}$.

"At least one success" (complementary event)
$$P(S_n\ge1)=1-(1-p)^n$$
complement
zero successes, i.e. all failures: $(1-p)^n$
Most likely number of successes not on CKE sheet
$$k_0=\lfloor (n+1)p\rfloor$$
note
when $(n+1)p$ is an integer, there are two: $(n+1)p$ and $(n+1)p-1$
Where does $(n+1)p$ come from? (compare neighboring terms)
$$\frac{P(S_n=k+1)}{P(S_n=k)}=\frac{(n-k)\,p}{(k+1)\,q}$$

This ratio is $\ge1$ if and only if $(n-k)p\ge(k+1)(1-p)$, i.e. $k\le(n+1)p-1$. The bars grow as long as $k+1\le(n+1)p$, then they shrink. On the exam it is safer to simply compute and compare a few terms around $np$.

2. Interactive: distribution of the number of successes

The bar above $k$ is $P(S_n=k)$. Change $n$ and $p$, choose an event, and the red bars add up to the result. The ▼ marker shows the most likely number of successes.

3. Simulation: the Galton board

At each peg the ball bounces right (success, $p$) or left. After $n$ pegs it lands in bin $k$ = the number of right bounces. The numbers at the pegs are the numbers of paths to them (Pascal's triangle, i.e. $\binom ik$). The bars are the frequencies from the simulation, the red dots are the theory.

4. Step-by-step example

A die is rolled $5$ times. Find the probability that a six comes up: a) exactly $2$ times, b) at least once.

  1. Success: a six, $p=\frac16$, $q=\frac56$, $n=5$.
  2. a) $P(S_5=2)=\binom52\left(\frac16\right)^2\left(\frac56\right)^3=10\cdot\frac{125}{7776}=\frac{1250}{7776}$.
  3. b) $P(S_5\ge1)=1-\left(\frac56\right)^5=1-\frac{3125}{7776}$.

a) $\frac{625}{3888}$, b) $\frac{4651}{7776}$

What is the minimum number of times a coin must be tossed so that the probability of getting at least one head is at least $0.99$?

  1. $1-\left(\frac12\right)^n\ge0.99 \iff 2^n\ge100$.
  2. $2^6=64<100$, $2^7=128\ge100$.

$n=7$

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    A coin is tossed $4$ times. Find the probability of getting exactly $2$ heads.
    Solution
    $\binom42\left(\frac12\right)^4=\frac{6}{16}$. $P=\frac38$
  2. easy
    A test has $6$ questions, each with $4$ answers (one correct). A student guesses. Find the probability that they answer exactly $3$ questions correctly.
    Solution
    $n=6$, $p=\frac14$: $\binom63\left(\frac14\right)^3\left(\frac34\right)^3=20\cdot\frac{27}{4096}=\frac{540}{4096}$. $P=\frac{135}{1024}$
  3. medium
    A marksman hits the target with probability $0.8$. He fires $5$ shots. Find the probability that he hits at least $4$ times.
    Solution
    $P(S_5=4)+P(S_5=5)=5\cdot0.8^4\cdot0.2+0.8^5=0.4096+0.32768$. $P=0.73728=\frac{2304}{3125}$
  4. medium
    What is the minimum number of times a die must be rolled so that the probability of rolling at least one six is greater than $\frac12$?
    Solution
    $1-\left(\frac56\right)^n>\frac12\iff\left(\frac56\right)^n<\frac12$. $\left(\frac56\right)^3=\frac{125}{216}\approx0.579$, $\left(\frac56\right)^4=\frac{625}{1296}\approx0.482$. $n=4$
  5. exam-level
    A die is rolled $20$ times. What number of sixes is the most likely?
    Solution
    $(n+1)p=\frac{21}{6}=3.5$, so $k_0=3$. Check with the ratio: $\frac{P(S=4)}{P(S=3)}=\frac{17\cdot\frac16}{4\cdot\frac56}=\frac{17}{20}<1$ and $\frac{P(S=3)}{P(S=2)}=\frac{18\cdot\frac16}{3\cdot\frac56}=\frac{6}{5}>1$. $k=3$
  6. exam-level
    In a scheme of $n$ trials with $p=\frac13$ the probability of exactly $2$ successes equals the probability of exactly $3$ successes. Find $n$.
    Solution
    $\binom n2\left(\frac13\right)^2\left(\frac23\right)^{n-2}=\binom n3\left(\frac13\right)^3\left(\frac23\right)^{n-3}$. Divide both sides by $\left(\frac13\right)^2\left(\frac23\right)^{n-3}$: $\binom n2\cdot\frac23=\binom n3\cdot\frac13$, i.e. $2\binom n2=\binom n3$. Hence $n(n-1)=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}$, $n-2=6$. $n=8$

6. On the exam

  • Write down the parameters: "success: …, $n=…$, $p=…$". Without this it is hard to get full marks, and it is easy to confuse $p$ with $q$.
  • The scheme works only for independent trials with the same $p$. Drawing without replacement is not Bernoulli (use combinations).
  • "At least one" → $1-q^n$. "At least two" → $1-P(S=0)-P(S=1)$.
  • A question about the minimum $n$: solve the inequality $q^n<\ldots$ by checking successive powers (logarithms are fine too). Give the smallest $n$.
  • Leave the result as a fraction, e.g. $\frac{625}{3888}$; do not round unless the task tells you to.
