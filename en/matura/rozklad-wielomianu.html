Factoring polynomials, integer roots

To factor a polynomial is to write it as a product of linear factors and quadratic factors with no roots. When nothing is obvious, you guess a root, but not blindly: an integer root must divide the constant term.

II.R.2, II.R.3

MethodExample
1. factor out the common factor$2x^4-6x^3=2x^3(x-3)$
2. special product formulas$x^4-16=(x^2-4)(x^2+4)$ $=(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$

Done when only factors of degree $1$ and trinomials with $\Delta<0$ remain (e.g. $x^2+4$, $x^2+x+1$).

Trick for grouping: when there are too few terms, split one of them: $$x^3-7x+6=x^3-x-6x+6$$ $$=x(x-1)(x+1)-6(x-1)$$ $$=(x-1)(x^2+x-6)$$ $$=(x-1)(x-2)(x+3)$$

2. Integer roots

Theorem If a polynomial $W(x)=a_nx^n+\ldots+a_1x+a_0$ with integer coefficients, $a_0\ne0$, has an integer root $k$, then $k$ is a divisor of the constant term $a_0$.
Proof

$W(k)=0$, that is, $a_nk^n+\ldots+a_1k+a_0=0$. Hence

$$a_0=-k\left(a_nk^{n-1}+\ldots+a_1\right)$$

The bracket is an integer (a sum of products of integers), so $a_0$ is a multiple of $k$, that is, $k\mid a_0$. $\blacksquare$

  • If $a_0=0$, first factor out $x$ (or $x^m$), and only then look for divisors.
  • The theorem gives only candidates. You check each one: is $W(k)=0$? Quick tests: $W(1)$ is the sum of the coefficients, and $W(-1)$ is easy to compute in your head too.
  • After finding $k$, divide by $x-k$ (Horner) and keep searching in the quotient, starting again from $k$: the root may be repeated.
  • When $a_n\ne\pm1$, the roots may be fractions, e.g. $2x-1$. The rational root theorem is outside the core curriculum; such roots will come out of $\Delta$ after the division.

3. Interactive: divisor sieve

Pick a polynomial or type integer coefficients (from the highest power). “Step →” tests the next candidate. A hit ($P(k)=0$) divides the polynomial by $x-k$; candidates that do not divide the new constant term drop out (grey). When a trinomial remains, $\Delta$ finishes the job.

4. Worked example

Factor $W(x)=x^4-x^3-7x^2+x+6$.

  1. Candidates: divisors of $6$: $\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm6$.
  2. $W(1)=1-1-7+1+6=0$. Horner for $1$: $\;1,\,0,\,-7,\,-6\;|\;0$, so $W(x)=(x-1)(x^3-7x-6)$.
  3. Quotient $P(x)=x^3-7x-6$: $P(1)=-12$, $P(-1)=-1+7-6=0$. Horner for $-1$: $\;1,\,-1,\,-6\;|\;0$.
  4. The trinomial $x^2-x-6$ remains: $\Delta=25$, roots $3$ and $-2$.

$W(x)=(x-1)(x+1)(x-3)(x+2)$

Faster: $W(1)=0$ is visible at once from the sum of the coefficients. Solving equations and inequalities using such a factorization: Polynomial equations and inequalities.

5. Tasks

  1. easy
    Factor $3x^5-12x^3$.
    Solution
    $3x^3(x^2-4)=$ $3x^3(x-2)(x+2)$
  2. easy
    Factor $x^3-5x^2-4x+20$ by grouping.
    Solution
    $x^2(x-5)-4(x-5)=(x-5)(x^2-4)=$ $(x-5)(x-2)(x+2)$
  3. medium
    Factor $x^4+x^3-8x-8$.
    Solution
    $x^3(x+1)-8(x+1)=(x+1)(x^3-8)$, and $x^3-8=(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)$, where $\Delta=-12<0$. $(x+1)(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)$
  4. medium
    Find the integer roots of the polynomial $W(x)=2x^3+x^2-13x+6$ and factor it.
    Solution
    Candidates: $\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm6$. $W(1)=-4$, $W(-1)=18$, $W(2)=16+4-26+6=0$. Horner for $2$: $\;2,\,5,\,-3\;|\;0$. $2x^2+5x-3$: $\Delta=49$, $x=-3$ or $x=\frac12$ (not an integer). Integer roots: $2$ and $-3$; $\;W(x)=(x-2)(x+3)(2x-1)$
  5. exam-level
    Prove that the polynomial $W(x)=x^3-3x+1$ has no integer roots.
    Solution
    The coefficients are integers, so an integer root would have to divide $a_0=1$, that is, it would equal $1$ or $-1$. But $W(1)=-1\ne0$ and $W(-1)=3\ne0$. No integer roots. $\blacksquare$
  6. exam-level
    The numbers $2$ and $-1$ are roots of the polynomial $W(x)=x^3+ax^2-4x+b$. Find $a$, $b$ and the third root.
    Solution
    $W(2)=4a+b=0$, $\;W(-1)=a+b+3=0$. Subtracting: $3a=3$, so $a=1$, $b=-4$. $W(x)=x^3+x^2-4x-4=x^2(x+1)-4(x+1)=(x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$. $a=1$, $b=-4$, third root $-2$

6. On the exam

  • Write down where the candidates come from (“an integer root divides the constant term $6$”) and show the computation $W(k)=0$. “I guessed” alone is not enough.
  • After Horner, do not stop at a trinomial with $\Delta\ge0$: factor it completely.
  • Always factor out the common factor first and check grouping: it often saves all the guessing.
  • Repeated root: after dividing by $x-k$, check $k$ once more in the quotient.
