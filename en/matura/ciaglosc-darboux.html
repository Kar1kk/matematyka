Continuity, the intermediate value property

A function continuous on an interval is a graph drawn without lifting the pencil. If such a graph starts below the axis and ends above it, it must cross the axis. This is the Darboux (intermediate value) property.

XIII.R.2, XIII.R.1

1. Continuity

Definition $f$ is continuous at a point $x_0$ of its domain when $\lim\limits_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)$. In practice: both one-sided limits exist and equal $f(x_0)$.
$$\lim_{x\to x_0^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0^+}f(x)=f(x_0)$$
  • Continuous on a set: continuous at each of its points (at the endpoints of a closed interval a one-sided limit is enough).
  • Polynomials, rational functions, roots, powers, $a^x$, $\log_a x$, $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$ are continuous on their domains. The sum, difference, product, quotient and composition of continuous functions are continuous too.
  • A discontinuity usually appears at the junction point of a piecewise function. There you compute one-sided limits.
$f(x)=\frac1x$ is continuous, because $0$ is not in the domain. But it is not continuous on the interval $[-1,1]$, because it is not even defined there.

2. Interactive: choose the parameter

$f(x)=x^2+1$ for $x\le1$ and $f(x)=ax-1$ for $x>1$. Change $a$ until the two pieces join.

3. The Darboux property

Theorem (Darboux property) If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a)\cdot f(b)<0$, then there is $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f(c)=0$.

More generally: a function continuous on $[a,b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.

  • The theorem gives existence, not the number of zeros. You justify uniqueness separately, e.g. by monotonicity.
  • For an equation $g(x)=h(x)$ you apply it to the difference $f(x)=g(x)-h(x)$.

Drag the endpoints $a$ and $b$ along the axis. When $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have different signs, a continuous function must cross the axis. "▶ Draw" moves the pencil from $a$ to $b$. Switch to $\frac1x$ (undefined at $0$) or a function with a jump: the signs differ, but there is no zero.

4. How to write it up on the exam

  1. Define $f$ (for an equation: $f(x)=\text{left}-\text{right}$) and write why it is continuous on $[a,b]$ (e.g. "as a polynomial").
  2. Compute $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, show that they have opposite signs.
  3. Conclusion: "By the Darboux property there is $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f(c)=0$".

5. Worked example

Justify that the equation $x^3+x-1=0$ has exactly one solution and that it lies in $(0,1)$.

  1. $f(x)=x^3+x-1$ is continuous on $\R$ (a polynomial).
  2. $f(0)=-1<0$, $f(1)=1>0$, so by the Darboux property there is $c\in(0,1)$, $f(c)=0$.
  3. Uniqueness: $x^3$ and $x-1$ are increasing, so $f$ is increasing and takes the value $0$ at most once.

The equation has exactly one solution, $c\in(0,1)$.

For which $m$ is the function $f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ for $x\neq2$, $f(2)=m$, continuous?

  1. Away from $2$ it is continuous (rational). We check only $x_0=2$.
  2. $\lim\limits_{x\to2}\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=\lim\limits_{x\to2}(x+2)=4$.
  3. Continuity at $2$: $f(2)=4$.

$m=4$

6. Exercises

  1. easy
    Investigate the continuity at $x_0=1$ of the function $f(x)=x+2$ for $x<1$, $f(x)=3x$ for $x\ge1$.
    Solution
    $\lim\limits_{x\to1^-}(x+2)=3$, $\lim\limits_{x\to1^+}3x=3$, $f(1)=3$. Continuous at $1$ (so on all of $\R$).
  2. medium
    Find $a$ for which the function $f(x)=ax+1$ for $x\le2$, $f(x)=x^2-a$ for $x>2$ is continuous.
    Solution
    Left-hand limit and $f(2)$: $2a+1$. Right-hand: $4-a$. Condition $2a+1=4-a$. $a=1$
  3. medium
    The function $f(x)=\frac{x^2-9}{x+3}$ for $x\neq-3$ and $f(-3)=k$. For which $k$ is it continuous?
    Solution
    $\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x+3}=x-3\to-6$. $k=-6$
  4. medium
    Justify that the equation $x^5-3x+1=0$ has a solution in the interval $(0,1)$.
    Solution
    $f(x)=x^5-3x+1$ is continuous (a polynomial). $f(0)=1>0$, $f(1)=-1<0$. By the Darboux property there is $c\in(0,1)$, $f(c)=0$.
  5. exam-level
    Justify that the equation $\cos x=x$ has a solution in the interval $\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
    Solution
    $f(x)=\cos x-x$ is continuous (a difference of continuous functions). $f(0)=1>0$, $f\left(\frac\pi2\right)=-\frac\pi2<0$. By the Darboux property there is $c\in\left(0,\frac\pi2\right)$, $\cos c=c$.
  6. exam-level
    Justify that the polynomial $W(x)=x^3-6x+2$ has three real roots.
    Solution
    $W$ is continuous. $W(-3)=-7$, $W(0)=2$, $W(1)=-3$, $W(3)=11$. The sign changes on $(-3,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,3)$, so each of these disjoint intervals contains a root (Darboux). A polynomial of degree $3$ has at most three. Exactly three real roots.

7. On the exam

  • Without a word about continuity the justification is incomplete. Write why $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.
  • Compute the values $f(a)$, $f(b)$ exactly and state the sign. Numbers alone without "$<0$", "$>0$" are not enough.
  • Darboux gives "at least one". "Exactly one" additionally requires monotonicity (often from the derivative).
  • Approximating a zero (bisection) is no longer in the core curriculum: it is enough to indicate an interval.
  • Continuity with a parameter: equate both one-sided limits and the value at the point.
